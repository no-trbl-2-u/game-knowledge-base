## Plot

- Traveling west alone, the kid shelters with a former slave trader who keeps a preserved human heart and warns about self-perpetuating human evil (PDF pp. 15–18).
- Cattle drovers feed the kid, give him beans, peppers, and a knife, and direct him toward Bexar (pp. 18–19).
- He enters Bexar amid signs of disease and asks a cantina keeper to exchange labor for liquor (pp. 19–21).
- When the keeper refuses payment after the work, the kid disables a pistol, attacks him with bottles, takes liquor, and leaves (pp. 21–22).
- He wakes in a ruined mission, observes bodies in the sacristy and damaged sacred figures, tracks his mule to the river, and bathes (pp. 22–24).

## Character Evidence

- **The kid:** Can negotiate and perform useful work, but responds to perceived breach with calculated, severe violence; he first neutralizes the firearm (pp. 20–22).
- **The hermit:** Offers shelter while presenting racist trophies and a theory that human invention can automate evil across generations (pp. 15–18).
- **The cattle drovers:** Extend material aid without interrogation, quietly adding supplies to the kid's mule (pp. 18–19).
- **The cantina keeper:** Initially permits the proposed work exchange, then dismisses the kid and reaches for a weapon when challenged (pp. 20–22).

## Voice & Narrative Techniques

- A journey structure links encounters that test hospitality, exchange, violence, and desecration.
- Dialogue carries competing moral theories without narratorial endorsement.
- Ruined religious imagery places institutional promise beside abandonment and bodily evidence.
- Language barriers become both comic friction and a catalyst for conflict.
- Recurring water imagery marks temporary survival rather than moral renewal.
