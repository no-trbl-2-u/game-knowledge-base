## Summary

Across the current evidence, deck-building is praised when it gives players legible control
over composition — deterministic no-shuffle sequencing, weighty single-card acquisitions,
a construction system fused tightly with another mechanism, or (now) a genuinely large
market that lets new players scale their own strategic ambition — and criticized when that
control feels shallow or arbitrary: thin starter decks, narrow markets, variance that reads
as "the deck failed me" rather than a player choice, low table interaction, or a combo system
whose handling cost outweighs its strategic payoff. Dominion finally supplies this pattern's
long-missing classic market-deckbuilder evidence: reviewers praise its quick turns and session
length and flag low player interaction and "expansion fatigue" as its clearest weaknesses —
a different failure shape from the campaign/LCG titles' starter-deck and variance complaints.

## Evidence by game

- dark-pact (src-008, src-010): some combo chains create more handling than strategic payoff ("lengthy combinations with weak returns"), confidence: medium
- dominion (src-002, src-003): many Kingdom-card choices let new players "choose how much strategy to apply," confidence: medium
- dominion (src-003): review's Final Thoughts "praises quick turns and the short session length", confidence: high
- dominion (src-003): Board Game Quest "explicitly lists lower player interaction as a minor flaw" while praising quick turns/accessibility, confidence: medium
- dominion (src-003, src-007): review lists "expansion fatigue" as a minor flaw; community errata article shows an ongoing, growing rules ecosystem, confidence: medium
- dune-imperium (src-001): BGG mechanism listing frames the game as fusing "Card Play Conflict Resolution" with "Deck, Bag, and Pool Building" as a single system rather than parallel tracks, confidence: high
- earthborne-rangers (src-004, src-005): Space-Biff praises the contextual card ecosystem, while Gideon's Gaming reports the subsystems built on top of it "can step on each other's toes", confidence: medium
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): A customer reviewer says "the card synergy is there but not deep enough that it takes too long to resolve," describing shallow but fast-resolving deck synergy, confidence: medium
- legendary-encounters-an-alien-deck-building-game (src-004): Commentary frames the game as "light deck-building fare" delivering "a string of simple but meaningful choices," confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-010): The Thoughtful Gamer calls it "a deckbuilder, but in slow motion," where "every time you acquire a new card it feels weighty and significant," confidence: high

- terraforming-mars (src-003): project cards are praised as a major source of variety and the engine-building identity, serving "functionally the same purpose" as patents while rewarding planning, confidence: high

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): No-shuffle deck construction turns losses into diagnosable planning errors rather than bad draws, which the reviewer treats as the game's core strength.
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006): Campaign deck construction across scenarios is called the best in the genre by a dedicated reviewer.
- mage-knight (src-010): Single-card acquisition is deliberately rare and heavy, making each deck addition feel earned.
- dune-imperium (src-001): Tying deck-building directly to the worker-placement action economy (rather than a separate acquisition phase) is highlighted as a defining, well-received design choice.
- dominion (src-002, src-003): a large Kingdom-card pool lets new players scale their own strategic ambition rather than being forced into full optimization immediately, alongside quick turns and short sessions being named as direct strengths.
- terraforming-mars (src-003): project-card variety is credited as the engine-building identity itself, rewarding planning rather than obscuring it.

## Where it fails

- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006): Original-core starter decks are "poor, unfocused," so new players can lose repeatedly before the construction system opens up — a common gap between "the engine is great" and "the first deck you're handed isn't."
- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): Deck composition variance can make a loss feel like the deck's fault rather than a player decision, even in an otherwise well-regarded system.
- aeons-end (src-005): Community comments describe the fixed market as offering limited perceived variety across plays.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): Card synergy is present but was described as not deep enough to reward extended optimization.
- dominion (src-003): low player interaction is named directly as a flaw in an otherwise accessible design, and a long-running expansion line has accumulated enough drift to earn an "expansion fatigue" complaint.
- dark-pact (src-008, src-010): some of the game's own combo chains cost more table time than the strategic payoff they deliver — a construction-complexity failure distinct from starter-deck weakness or market narrowness.
- earthborne-rangers (src-004, src-005): a praised contextual card ecosystem can still have its subsystems interfere with each other as more of them layer on.

## Coverage gaps

Dominion now supplies this pattern's long-missing classic competitive market-deckbuilder
evidence, sharpening the "market variety vs. narrow market" side with a genuinely different
failure shape (low interaction, expansion fatigue) from the campaign/LCG titles' starter-deck
and variance complaints. The remaining gap is a market-deckbuilder whose reviewers specifically
discuss deck-thinning mechanics or market-slot competition between players, since Dominion's
cited evidence here is about pacing, interaction, and product-line fatigue rather than the
market-economy mechanics themselves.
