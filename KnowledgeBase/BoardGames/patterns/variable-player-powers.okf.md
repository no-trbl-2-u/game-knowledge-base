## Summary

Reviewers consistently credit character, faction, or hero variety with replayability — Marvel Champions' hero/villain combinatorics, Heroes of Terrinoth's hero variety, Too Many Bones' Gearloc builds, Cthulhu: Death May Die's Elder One/scenario/investigator combos, Terra Mystica's fourteen factions, Star Wars: Imperial Assault's Imperial-versus-heroes campaign structure, and Unmatched's fighter cast (called "absolutely the best thing about Unmatched") all name asymmetric powers as a central strength. Scythe adds a balance-focused variant of the same claim: faction/mat/starting-location asymmetry is praised specifically because no single faction felt overpowered. But the same asymmetry that drives replayability is what makes these games harder to learn — each kit or faction is effectively its own ruleset (Root), and a build only "clicks" after a real learning-curve investment (Too Many Bones). Asymmetry-as-replayability and asymmetry-as-onboarding-tax read as the same design coin rather than competing findings, and the majority of the new evidence this pass is about the replayability face of that coin rather than its onboarding cost.

## Where it works

- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): hero/villain combinatorics are named as the game's central replayability driver, confidence: high
- terra-mystica (src-003): asymmetric faction powers are credited as the strategic core of a low-luck, high-strategy design, confidence: high
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): rules-bendy, character-specific fighter design is singled out as the single best thing about the game, confidence: medium
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): once learned, Gearloc build distinctiveness and character-specific dice are the game's clearest praised strength, confidence: medium

## Where it fails

- root (src-004): onboarding cost scales with faction count — "each faction is its own lesson", not a shared ruleset with variants, confidence: high
- root (src-006): the skill ceiling created by asymmetry means players must learn opposing factions' engines, not just their own, to play well, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): the same character-specific build depth that earns praise also produces a learning curve that reviewers say may deter casual or younger players, confidence: medium

## Coverage gaps

The evidence base has broadened considerably on the replayability side (Cthulhu: Death May Die, Descent, Elder Sign, Scythe, Star Wars: Imperial Assault, Terra Mystica, and Unmatched all now contribute), but "Where it fails" still rests on only two games, Root and Too Many Bones. Several strong asymmetric-power designs (Spirit Island, Gloomhaven, Arkham Horror: The Card Game, Mage Knight) still carry the `variable-player-powers` mechanics tag without a source-attributed claim specifically about asymmetric powers rather than campaign length, setup, or general rules load, so they remain excluded rather than forced in. Too Many Bones still carries `needs_followup` on its reception doc, which keeps this doc's status at `draft`. The corpus would still benefit from a game whose reviewers explicitly discuss asymmetric-character *onboarding* trade-offs in detail (e.g., a large-roster hero game with commentary on learning one class vs. learning all of them) to deepen the "where it fails" side beyond Root and Too Many Bones.
