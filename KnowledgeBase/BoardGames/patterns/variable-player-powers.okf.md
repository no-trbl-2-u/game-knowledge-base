## Summary

Evidence was thin (4 of 15 candidate games) and has grown to 9. Where evidence exists, the pattern is consistent: reviewers credit character/hero/faction variety directly with replayability (Marvel Champions, Heroes of Terrinoth, Too Many Bones, Elder Sign, Unmatched), but the same asymmetry is what makes the game harder to learn — either because each kit is its own lesson (Root) or because a build only "clicks" after a real learning-curve investment (Too Many Bones). Asymmetry-as-replayability and asymmetry-as-onboarding-tax appear to be the same coin, not competing findings. Betrayal Legacy and Star Wars: Imperial Assault add a third shape: asymmetry praised for building real player/faction *identity* (not just build variety), while Terra Mystica shows that a large asymmetric roster (14 factions) can be engineered toward deterministic balance rather than left to chance.

## Evidence by game

- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): reviewer frames hero/villain combinations as the central replayability signal — "With nearly unlimited combinations of notable heroes and villainous schemes...", confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): reviewer's Pros list credits hero variety (alongside upgrades, randomized quest sets, and dice) with the game's replay value, confidence: high
- root (src-004): learning Root "demands more than just memorising the general rules" because each faction is effectively its own ruleset to learn, confidence: high
- root (src-006): reaching proficiency requires understanding not just your own faction but every other faction at the table — asymmetry raises the mastery ceiling for everyone, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): reviewers praise the depth and distinctiveness of Gearloc-specific builds and treat the character-specific dice as tactical identity, but both reviews also flag a real learning curve and complexity that may deter casual players, confidence: medium
- betrayal-legacy (src-006): a reviewer credits the design with "building identities for the players at the table" through persistent, character-specific narrative choices across the legacy campaign, confidence: high
- elder-sign (src-002, src-004): the combination of characters, Great Old Ones, and adventures is credited with "a good deal of replayability," while a separate claim notes the game plays slightly easier with more players since extra characters bring extra special powers to spread damage across, confidence: high
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): the review frames the entire design around one Imperial player against 1–4 Rebel heroes as a strength, an asymmetry of role and scale rather than just differing abilities, confidence: high
- terra-mystica (src-003, src-004): Board Game Quest calls the 14-faction system "low-luck and high-strategy" with fully deterministic victory parameters, framing broad asymmetric variety as something engineered toward fairness rather than left to chance, confidence: high
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): the reviewer calls the character roster "absolutely the best thing about Unmatched," crediting familiar archetypes made "rules-bendy and flexible" as the source of replayable variety, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): hero/villain combinatorics are named as the game's central replayability driver, confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): hero variety is listed alongside upgrades and randomized content as a source of replay value, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): once learned, Gearloc build distinctiveness and character-specific dice are the game's clearest praised strength, confidence: medium
- betrayal-legacy (src-006): persistent, character-specific narrative choices are credited with building genuine player identity across a legacy campaign — asymmetry as identity, not just mechanical variety.
- elder-sign (src-002): the combination of characters, Great Old Ones, and adventures is named directly as a source of replayability.
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): the character roster is called "absolutely the best thing" about the game, crediting familiar archetypes turned "rules-bendy and flexible."
- terra-mystica (src-003, src-004): 14 factions are praised as achieving "low-luck and high-strategy" balance through deterministic victory parameters, rather than asymmetry being left to chance.

## Where it fails

- root (src-004): onboarding cost scales with faction count — "each faction is its own lesson", not a shared ruleset with variants, confidence: high
- root (src-006): the skill ceiling created by asymmetry means players must learn opposing factions' engines, not just their own, to play well, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): the same character-specific build depth that earns praise also produces a learning curve that reviewers say may deter casual or younger players, confidence: medium
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-003): campaign-level balance concerns are raised specifically about how the asymmetric one-vs-many structure plays out over a full campaign, even as the base structure is praised.

## Coverage gaps

Several candidate games (including strong asymmetric-power designs like Spirit Island, Gloomhaven, Arkham Horror: The Card Game, and Mage Knight) carry the `variable-player-powers` mechanics tag but did not yield a claim in their own reception docs that was clearly, specifically about asymmetric powers rather than campaign length, setup, or rules load in general — their "Praised design" sections gesture at asymmetric spirits/investigators/classes but without a source-attributed quote to cite here, so those games were left out rather than forced in. Kingdom Death: Monster and Too Many Bones also still carry `needs_followup` on their reception docs (Too Many Bones' evidence above is included but keeps this doc's status at `draft`; Kingdom Death: Monster had no variable-player-powers-specific claim at all). The corpus still lacks a game whose reviewers explicitly discuss asymmetric-character *onboarding* trade-offs in the specific sense of "learning one class vs. learning all of them" — none of this pass's additions supply that angle either, so the "where it fails" side remains anchored on Root and Too Many Bones.
