## Summary

Only 6 of the 22 candidate games yielded reception evidence genuinely about variable/asymmetric powers as a design feature (as opposed to just carrying the mechanics tag). Where evidence exists, the pattern is consistent: reviewers credit character/hero/faction variety directly with replayability (Marvel Champions, Heroes of Terrinoth, Too Many Bones, Descent 2E, Star Wars: Imperial Assault), but the same asymmetry is what makes the game harder to learn — either because each kit is its own lesson (Root) or because a build only "clicks" after a real learning-curve investment (Too Many Bones). Descent 2E and Imperial Assault add a campaign-specific variant of the replayability side: a distinct one-vs-many opposing role (Overlord, Imperial player) is itself the praised asymmetric structure, not a roster of interchangeable heroes. Asymmetry-as-replayability and asymmetry-as-onboarding-tax appear to be the same coin, not competing findings.

## Evidence by game

- descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition (src-001, src-003): BGG and the publisher both foreground the Overlord-versus-heroes role split as the campaign's defining asymmetric structure and central selling point, confidence: high
- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): reviewer frames hero/villain combinations as the central replayability signal — "With nearly unlimited combinations of notable heroes and villainous schemes...", confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): reviewer's Pros list credits hero variety (alongside upgrades, randomized quest sets, and dice) with the game's replay value, confidence: high
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): reviewer frames the entire game around giving one player a distinct opposing (Imperial) role against 1-4 individually-played Rebel heroes, confidence: high
- root (src-004): learning Root "demands more than just memorising the general rules" because each faction is effectively its own ruleset to learn, confidence: high
- root (src-006): reaching proficiency requires understanding not just your own faction but every other faction at the table — asymmetry raises the mastery ceiling for everyone, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): reviewers praise the depth and distinctiveness of Gearloc-specific builds and treat the character-specific dice as tactical identity, but both reviews also flag a real learning curve and complexity that may deter casual players, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): hero/villain combinatorics are named as the game's central replayability driver, confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): hero variety is listed alongside upgrades and randomized content as a source of replay value, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): once learned, Gearloc build distinctiveness and character-specific dice are the game's clearest praised strength, confidence: medium
- descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition (src-001, src-003): the Overlord-versus-heroes split is treated as the campaign's defining asymmetric strength rather than a roster of palette-swapped heroes, confidence: high
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): a structurally distinct opposing role (the Imperial player) against individual Rebel heroes is treated as a successful, central design choice, confidence: high

## Where it fails

- root (src-004): onboarding cost scales with faction count — "each faction is its own lesson", not a shared ruleset with variants, confidence: high
- root (src-006): the skill ceiling created by asymmetry means players must learn opposing factions' engines, not just their own, to play well, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): the same character-specific build depth that earns praise also produces a learning curve that reviewers say may deter casual or younger players, confidence: medium

## Coverage gaps

Eleven of the original fifteen candidate games (including strong asymmetric-power designs like Spirit Island, Gloomhaven, Arkham Horror: The Card Game, and Mage Knight) carry the `variable-player-powers` mechanics tag but did not yield a claim in their own reception docs that was clearly, specifically about asymmetric powers rather than campaign length, setup, or rules load in general — their "Praised design" sections gesture at asymmetric spirits/investigators/classes but without a source-attributed quote to cite here, so those games were left out rather than forced in. arydia-the-paths-we-dare-tread, betrayal-legacy, cthulhu-death-may-die, nemesis, and tainted-grail-the-fall-of-avalon also now carry the tag, but their reception docs discuss campaign structure, legacy mechanics, progression tracks, or hidden objectives rather than power/faction asymmetry specifically, so they were excluded too. Kingdom Death: Monster and Too Many Bones also still carry `needs_followup` on their reception docs (Too Many Bones' evidence above is included but keeps this doc's status at `draft`; Kingdom Death: Monster had no variable-player-powers-specific claim at all). The corpus would benefit from a game whose reviewers explicitly discuss asymmetric-character *onboarding* trade-offs in detail (e.g., a large-roster hero game with reviewer commentary on learning one class vs. learning all of them) to deepen the "where it fails" side beyond Root and Too Many Bones.
