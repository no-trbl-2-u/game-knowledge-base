## Summary

Only 5 of the now much larger set of candidate games yielded reception evidence genuinely about variable/asymmetric powers as a design feature (as opposed to just carrying the mechanics tag). Where evidence exists, the pattern is consistent: reviewers credit character/hero/faction variety directly with replayability (Marvel Champions, Heroes of Terrinoth, Too Many Bones), but the same asymmetry is what makes the game harder to learn — either because each kit is its own lesson (Root), a build only "clicks" after a real learning-curve investment (Too Many Bones), or the game literally splits players into structurally different roles that each need their own teach (Star Wars: Imperial Assault's Imperial-vs-Rebel divide). Asymmetry-as-replayability and asymmetry-as-onboarding-tax appear to be the same coin, not competing findings.

## Evidence by game

- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): reviewer frames hero/villain combinations as the central replayability signal — "With nearly unlimited combinations of notable heroes and villainous schemes...", confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): reviewer's Pros list credits hero variety (alongside upgrades, randomized quest sets, and dice) with the game's replay value, confidence: high
- root (src-004): learning Root "demands more than just memorising the general rules" because each faction is effectively its own ruleset to learn, confidence: high
- root (src-006): reaching proficiency requires understanding not just your own faction but every other faction at the table — asymmetry raises the mastery ceiling for everyone, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): reviewers praise the depth and distinctiveness of Gearloc-specific builds and treat the character-specific dice as tactical identity, but both reviews also flag a real learning curve and complexity that may deter casual players, confidence: medium
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): the review presents a multi-role tactical campaign where the Imperial player and Rebel heroes are structurally distinct, supporting teaching the two roles through a short guided mission rather than one shared rules pass, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- marvel-champions-the-card-game (src-004): hero/villain combinatorics are named as the game's central replayability driver, confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): hero variety is listed alongside upgrades and randomized content as a source of replay value, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): once learned, Gearloc build distinctiveness and character-specific dice are the game's clearest praised strength, confidence: medium

## Where it fails

- root (src-004): onboarding cost scales with faction count — "each faction is its own lesson", not a shared ruleset with variants, confidence: high
- root (src-006): the skill ceiling created by asymmetry means players must learn opposing factions' engines, not just their own, to play well, confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): the same character-specific build depth that earns praise also produces a learning curve that reviewers say may deter casual or younger players, confidence: medium
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): a structurally asymmetric two-side campaign (one Imperial player vs. up to four Rebel heroes) means onboarding cost effectively doubles — two distinct rule perspectives need teaching, not one shared ruleset with variant flavor.

## Coverage gaps

A large majority of the corpus's `variable-player-powers`-tagged games (including strong asymmetric-power designs like Spirit Island, Gloomhaven, Arkham Horror: The Card Game, Mage Knight, and most of this pass's new campaign-RPG dossiers — Scythe, Cthulhu: Death May Die, Descent, Tainted Grail, Nemesis, Arydia, Horrified, Elder Sign) carry the `variable-player-powers` mechanics tag but did not yield a claim in their own reception docs that was clearly, specifically about asymmetric powers rather than campaign length, setup, balance, or rules load in general — their "Praised design" sections gesture at asymmetric spirits/investigators/classes but without a source-attributed quote to cite here, so those games were left out rather than forced in. Kingdom Death: Monster and Too Many Bones also still carry `needs_followup` on their reception docs (Too Many Bones' evidence above is included but keeps this doc's status at `draft`; Kingdom Death: Monster had no variable-player-powers-specific claim at all). Star Wars: Imperial Assault's contribution narrows the "where it fails" gap by adding a two-*sided* (not just multi-character) asymmetric onboarding case. The corpus would still benefit from a game whose reviewers explicitly discuss asymmetric-character onboarding trade-offs within a single team (learning one class vs. learning all of them at the same table) to deepen that side beyond Root and Too Many Bones.
