## Summary

Hidden information consistently reads as an engagement multiplier rather than a source of confusion or unfairness. A hidden Crossroads deck is called "brilliant" for the "weight and extraordinary moments" it creates (Dead of Winter: A Crossroads Game); private objectives generate mutual suspicion while still preserving each player's incentive to cooperate (Nemesis); and traitor uncertainty drives interaction throughout Shadows over Camelot even when reviewers can't be sure a traitor is actually in play, with one reviewer naming that "no-guaranteed-Traitor social layer" the single best part of the game. The Crew complicates the picture: its restricted-communication rule (no table talk, a single communication token per mission) is cited as both a genuine friction point and, in the same breath, the source of the tension that makes its cooperation interesting — hidden/restricted information can be simultaneously the complaint and the payoff for the same design.

- the-crew (src-002, src-003): that same constrained communication "is part of the game's tension" — the restriction is also praised as what makes the cooperation interesting, confidence: high

## Where it works

- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): the hidden Crossroads deck is "brilliant," producing weight and extraordinary moments that a fully open information state could not.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): uncertainty over whether a Traitor is even present sustains suspicion and accusation as the game's social centerpiece, independent of whether treachery is actually dealt.
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): private objectives generate mutual suspicion between players without breaking the shared incentive to survive.

## Where it fails

- the-crew (src-003, src-004): the no-table-talk rule and single-use communication token are named as a genuine point of friction for players used to freer cooperative communication.

## Coverage gaps

The Crew is the only game in this set where hidden/restricted information is cited as a friction point rather than a pure strength, and even there the same restriction is praised elsewhere (src-002, src-003) as the source of the game's tension — the corpus lacks a case where restricted communication was reported as an unambiguous failure rather than a double-edged design choice. This pattern also overlaps heavily with semi-cooperative-game: Dead of Winter and Shadows over Camelot supply evidence for both, since the traitor/exposure mechanics that create semi-cooperative suspicion are themselves the hidden-information device. A game whose reviewers discuss hidden information in a fully competitive (non-cooperative) context would help separate this pattern from its current entanglement with cooperative and semi-cooperative design.
