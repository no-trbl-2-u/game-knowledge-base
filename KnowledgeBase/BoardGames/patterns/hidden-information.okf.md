## Summary

New this pass: hidden-information crosses this corpus's "5+ games with genuine
body evidence" bar for the first time, after sitting as a thin, deferred tag in
earlier passes. Where reviewers engage with hidden information directly, the
pattern is consistent and positive: a private objective, a secretly-kept card,
or a restricted-communication channel is repeatedly named as *the* source of
tension and engagement, more often than not praised by name rather than merely
tolerated. The one partial exception is Betrayal Legacy, where hidden card-draw
variance (rather than a hidden role or objective) is read as randomness
overriding player agency instead of generating productive uncertainty — the
same underlying mechanic (information withheld from the acting player) reads
differently depending on whether what's hidden is a *choice* (an objective, a
card in another player's hand) or an *outcome* (what the next card draw will be).

## Evidence by game

- betrayal-legacy (src-005): the reviewer describes card draws that can leave a player
  "unable to even meaningfully participate" despite reasonable play, confidence: medium
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): the review calls the Crossroads card
  mechanism "brilliant," names it as its favorite part, and says the hidden cards
  "create weight and extraordinary moments," confidence: high
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): reviews repeatedly identify two private hidden
  objectives — not a conventional revealed-traitor structure — as the actual tension
  source, contrasting public cooperation with private agenda, confidence: high
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): traitor-identity uncertainty is identified
  as what drives interaction, with reviewers praising the accusation mechanism by name,
  confidence: high
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): the manual's one-token-per-mission communication limit
  (keeping hand contents effectively hidden) is credited by a reviewer with creating
  tension rather than frustration, confidence: high

## Where it works

- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): hidden Crossroads cards are called the
  reviewer's favorite part of the game by name, crediting the hidden-information design
  directly with the game's best moments.
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): private hidden objectives are named directly as
  the source of tension across three independent reviews — the strongest "named by
  multiple sources" praise for a hidden-information design in this corpus.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): hidden-traitor uncertainty is singled out by
  name as what makes the social layer work.
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): a strict communication limit that keeps hand contents
  effectively hidden from teammates is credited with creating tension rather than being
  read as a restriction.

## Where it fails

- betrayal-legacy (src-005): hidden card-draw variance can leave an attentive player
  locked out of meaningful participation regardless of play quality — hidden *outcomes*
  reading as imposed randomness rather than productive uncertainty.

## Coverage gaps

Four of the five cited games frame hidden information as a hidden *choice or goal*
(an objective, a role, a hand of cards) and are praised for it; only Betrayal Legacy
frames it as a hidden *outcome* (the next card drawn) and is criticized for it. That
split is based on a single game on the "fails" side, so it should be read as a
hypothesis, not a settled finding. Three of the five cited games
(betrayal-legacy, dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game, shadows-over-camelot) have
reception docs still `status: needs_followup`, which keeps this doc's own status at
`draft`. A heavier negotiation or deduction game with a `verified` reception doc and
an explicit hidden-role complaint (rather than praise) would help test whether the
hidden-choice/hidden-outcome split holds up or is an artifact of this pass's sample.
