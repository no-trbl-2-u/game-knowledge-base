## Summary

`modular-board` has just crossed the five-game tag-prevalence threshold, but
only three of those games have reviewer commentary that substantively engages
with the modular board itself rather than treating it as background metadata
— evidence is thin relative to how widely the tag is applied. The three games
that do comment on it split sharply, and not along a simple works/fails line
for the mechanic overall: nemesis's randomized room-tile layout is praised for
the same property — unpredictable, changing arrangement — that
cthulhu-death-may-die and descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition each
complain about, but for unrelated reasons. Cthulhu: Death May Die's complaint
is a physical component-fit problem (miniatures don't fit the tile spaces);
Descent's complaint is a pacing/reveal problem (setting up the full board at
once removes the sense of unknown territory). So the single evidenced
"works" case and the two "fails" cases are not disagreeing about whether
modularity itself is good — they're flagging that the same design choice
(assemble the board from interchangeable pieces) creates distinct risks
depending on what the modularity is used for: replayability support (works)
versus component logistics and exploration pacing (fails).

## Evidence by game

- nemesis (src-007, src-008, src-009): randomized room-tile layout creates "changing chaotic layouts" credited as a driver of replayability, confidence: high
- cthulhu-death-may-die (src-003): "you will run out of room for miniatures on some of the tiles" — the modular tile layout creates a physical component-fit problem, confidence: high
- descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition (src-006): setting up the full modular board from the start gives "no real feeling of mystery" — the modularity's exploration payoff is undercut by full up-front reveal, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- nemesis (src-007, src-008, src-009): the randomized, tile-based room layout produces "changing chaotic layouts" from game to game, which reviewers credit as a direct driver of replayability rather than a neutral setup mechanism.

## Where it fails

- cthulhu-death-may-die (src-003): the modular tile layout is not sized for the game's own miniatures — "you will run out of room for miniatures on some of the tiles" — a component-clarity failure of the modular pieces themselves, not of the randomization.
- descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition (src-006): assembling the full modular board up front, rather than revealing tiles as the party explores, leaves "no real feeling of mystery" — the same modularity that could support incremental discovery is used here in a way that forecloses it.

## Coverage gaps

Only three of the five (or more) `modular-board`-tagged games have reviewer
commentary that engages the mechanic directly; the remaining tagged games
discuss other systems and leave the modular board itself uncited. The two
"fails" cases here are not actually about modularity being disliked in
principle — they're a component-sizing complaint and a setup-procedure
complaint that happen to attach to modular boards. The corpus would benefit
from a game whose reviewers discuss modular-board setup procedure explicitly
(progressive tile reveal vs. full up-front assembly) to test whether Descent's
complaint is a general risk of the mechanic or specific to that game's
scripted setup.
