## Summary

Onboarding friction now shows up in three distinct shapes across this corpus (36 games). Rules-cliff games make the first session teach a whole engine at once (Root's faction opacity, Mage Knight's "obtuse" rulebook, Star Wars Rebellion's "large learning investment," War of the Ring's dice-action overload). Campaign games pair staged deck/character construction with a punishing early loss (Arkham Horror LCG's weak starter decks, Aeon's End's no-shuffle sequencing, Too Many Bones' walkthrough dependency, Nemesis's twenty-step setup). A third shape is distinct from both: heavy euro-engine games (Gaia Project, Terra Mystica, Scythe, Twilight Imperium, SETI) where reviewers call the base rules themselves simple or "straightforward," but the real barrier is the interlocking strategic grammar — action economy, strategy-card interactions, tech/resource conversions — that only becomes legible after a game or two. Across all three shapes, the recurring fix reviewers point to is not simplification but sequencing: an official first-game path (Gloomhaven: Jaws of the Lion's five-scenario tutorial, Arkham Horror LCG's Learn to Play scenario), a scripted teach round, or deferring the strategic grammar past session one. A handful of high-confidence claims now exist (Root, Mage Knight, Scythe, Star Wars Rebellion, Gloomhaven: Jaws of the Lion, Shadows over Camelot, War of the Ring) alongside the earlier single-review inferences, so this is no longer purely a thin-evidence pattern.

## Where it works

- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-005): Board Game Quest characterizes the system as "a deckbuilding game with campaign elements and a progressive narrative" — the upgrade cadence introduces construction gradually rather than all at once.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): customer reviews praise that "the base game is easy to learn" while expansions layer in additional elements, letting complexity ramp after the first sessions.
- gloomhaven-jaws-of-the-lion (src-001, src-003): the publisher's five-scenario tutorial is the clearest "solves onboarding well" example in the corpus — both the publisher and an independent reviewer credit the staged ramp, not just low complexity.
- dominion (src-002, src-003): letting new players "choose how much strategy to apply" turns the large Kingdom-card count into an onboarding lever instead of a wall.
- the-crew (src-002, src-004): the mission deck's own difficulty curve does onboarding work before any external aid is needed.

## Where it fails

- root (src-004, reviews.okf.md player friction): "each faction is its own lesson" — a player can know the shared rules and still not understand the game state because opponents' faction engines stay opaque.
- mage-knight (src-010): the review's own framing — obtuse and most complicated before it clicks — shows the rulebook itself as the barrier, independent of the underlying design's quality.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): rulebook assumptions about video-game familiarity leave non-video-game players stranded at specific junctures (deck-shuffling exceptions, post-Act-III progression).
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006, via better-if.okf.md): "The player decks provided are poor, unfocused," making early losses feel punishing before the deck-construction engine that justifies the system opens up.
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): the learning curve is steep enough that reviewers treat an external walkthrough as a near-required onboarding aid.
- scythe (src-003): veteran players' faster paths to early stars leave new players structurally behind in their very first game.
- star-wars-rebellion (src-005, src-006) and war-of-the-ring (src-008): both reviewers describe a "large learning investment" or first-timers "overwhelmed" by dice actions even with reference pages in hand — long, exception-heavy war-game rulesets resist the staged-tutorial fix that works for campaign games.
- nemesis (src-005, src-008): a twenty-step setup before the first decision point front-loads friction that a staged teach could defer.

## Coverage gaps

The sample now spans rules-cliff, campaign, and heavy-euro shapes, and several claims carry high confidence rather than single-review inference alone. The remaining gap is the same one as before: a widely-praised "teaches itself" gateway game credited for solving onboarding well (not just avoiding the problem by being simple) would balance the "Where it works" section, which is still thinner than "Where it fails." ark-nova and kingdom-death-monster still carry the onboarding label in frontmatter without a distinct fetched claim, so they remain excluded.
