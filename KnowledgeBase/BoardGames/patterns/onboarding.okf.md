## Summary

Onboarding friction shows up in three distinct shapes across this corpus: rules-cliff games where the first session must teach a whole engine at once (Root's faction opacity, Mage Knight's "obtuse" rulebook, Slay the Spire's video-game-literate assumptions), campaign games where the friction is staged deck/character construction meeting a punishing early loss (Arkham Horror LCG's weak starter decks, Aeon's End's no-shuffle sequencing, Too Many Bones' walkthrough dependency), and a documentation-vs-design gap where the underlying ruleset teaches faster in conversation than its printed materials suggest (Shadows over Camelot's 10-15 minute oral teach against "two beautiful books" that are "much longer and more detailed than necessary"). The recurring fix reviewers point to is not simplification but sequencing: an official first-game path, a scripted teach round, or a gradual-unlock structure that defers full complexity past session one. Evidence is broad (21 games) but shallow per game — most claims are single-review inferences rather than dense player-comment consensus.

## Where it works

- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-005): Board Game Quest characterizes the system as "a deckbuilding game with campaign elements and a progressive narrative" — the upgrade cadence introduces construction gradually rather than all at once.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): customer reviews praise that "the base game is easy to learn" while expansions layer in additional elements, letting complexity ramp after the first sessions.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006): the base ruleset itself is teachable in ten to fifteen minutes through conversation — the design's actual rules-load is smaller than its printed documentation suggests, a rare case where the underlying game, not just a review, points at documentation as the bottleneck rather than the rules themselves.

## Where it fails

- root (src-004, reviews.okf.md player friction): "each faction is its own lesson" — a player can know the shared rules and still not understand the game state because opponents' faction engines stay opaque.
- mage-knight (src-010): the review's own framing — obtuse and most complicated before it clicks — shows the rulebook itself as the barrier, independent of the underlying design's quality.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): rulebook assumptions about video-game familiarity leave non-video-game players stranded at specific junctures (deck-shuffling exceptions, post-Act-III progression).
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006, via better-if.okf.md): "The player decks provided are poor, unfocused," making early losses feel punishing before the deck-construction engine that justifies the system opens up.
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): the learning curve is steep enough that reviewers treat an external walkthrough as a near-required onboarding aid.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006): two "beautiful" but overlong rulebooks outweigh the game's actual oral-teach time, and missing the separate Quest-book rules is called out as an easy mistake.
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): rules length itself is named as a barrier, requiring a group with existing rules familiarity rather than a scripted first play.
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): the substantial scenario and dual-role campaign structure implies a real per-role teaching burden before a first mission can run smoothly.

## Coverage gaps

Nearly every citation here comes from a single review's framing rather than dense multi-source consensus, and the sample skews toward heavy campaign/deckbuilding games — ark-nova and kingdom-death-monster carry the onboarding label in their frontmatter but their fetched text didn't yield an onboarding-specific claim distinct from general weight/scale, so they were excluded. Tainted Grail: The Fall of Avalon's contribution rests on review metadata (a complexity rating) rather than quoted reviewer prose, so it should be treated as the weakest citation in this table pending a fuller source pass (its doc is still `needs_followup`). The corpus would benefit from a lighter, widely-praised "teaches itself" gateway game (something reviewers credit for solving onboarding well, not just avoiding the problem by being simple) to give the "Where it works" side more than three data points.
