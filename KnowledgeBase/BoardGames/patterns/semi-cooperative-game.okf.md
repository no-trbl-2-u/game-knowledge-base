## Summary

Ten games in the corpus carry the `semi-cooperative-game` mechanics tag, but only three have reception commentary that actually engages with the semi-cooperative dynamic itself rather than carrying the tag as background metadata — thin evidence relative to tag prevalence. Where reviewers do engage with it, semi-cooperation reads as a source of engagement rather than friction: private objectives and exposure risk layered on top of a shared goal (Dead of Winter: A Crossroads Game) and persistent mutual suspicion even in variants where a hidden traitor may not be in play at all (Shadows over Camelot) both get credited with driving player interaction. The one neutral data point, Bloodborne: The Card Game, suggests the take-that/nastiness tension that makes semi-cooperation interesting is table-dependent rather than a universal win.

## Evidence by game

- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): take-that/nastiness "worked for the reviewer" but is explicitly flagged as group-dependent — the semi-cooperative/competitive tension needs the right table, confidence: high
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): private objectives, betrayal risk, and exposure mechanics combine into "a seriously engaging experience" — hidden allegiance layered on top of cooperation, confidence: high
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006): suspicion persists "even when no Traitor was dealt" — the mere possibility of a hidden traitor drives interaction even in games without one, confidence: high

## Where it works

- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): private objectives, betrayal risk, and exposure combine into what the reviewer calls "a seriously engaging experience."
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006): the uncertainty of whether a Traitor is even in play keeps players suspicious of each other throughout, independent of whether treachery is actually present.

## Where it fails

No game in the current evidence set reports a semi-cooperative design that players actively disliked; Bloodborne: The Card Game's take-that tension is reported as group-dependent rather than a failure. A title with an explicit semi-cooperative failure case — a group that found the accusation/suspicion layer unpleasant rather than merely table-dependent — would give this section real content.

## Coverage gaps

Semi-cooperative-game heavily overlaps with the hidden-information pattern in this corpus: Dead of Winter and Shadows over Camelot supply evidence for both docs, since the traitor-uncertainty mechanic that makes semi-cooperation engaging is itself a hidden-information device. Seven of the ten tagged games have no reviewer commentary that engages with the semi-cooperative dynamic specifically rather than mentioning the tag in passing. The clearest gap is a documented failure case — a reviewer or group that actively disliked the accusation/suspicion layer, rather than one that merely found it group-dependent — to balance "Where it works" against a real "Where it fails."
