## Summary

Randomness friction splits into two failure modes and one recurring fix. Too much
uncontrolled variance (an oversized card pool, an artificial catch-up bump, a
dice-gated resolution with no deterministic recourse) reads as the game overriding
player skill; too little variance (a thin card pool that repeats) reads as
predictable and stale. A third shape recurs specifically in cooperative/survival
games (Nemesis, Horrified): the same chaos that produces memorable emergent stories
is, in the same breath, criticized as overwhelming or domino-like when it determines
the outcome rather than the narrative texture — these are not contradictory claims
but a confidence/control split between "good randomness for story" and "bad
randomness for winning." The recurring mitigation reviewers reward is giving players
a lever over the randomness — deterministic ordering, a dice-or-card choice, a
bounded/legible puzzle per randomized element (Horrified's per-monster objective), or
a catch-up tool framed as earned rather than free — so a bad outcome still traces
back to a legible decision.

## Evidence by game

- aeons-end (src-004): no-shuffle deck ordering is praised because it turns deck-out
  losses into accountable planning failures rather than bad draws ("it wasn't a bad
  draw, it was a bad choice"), though onboarding into that discipline is itself a
  friction point, confidence: high
- ark-nova (src-008): the 212-card zoo deck produces "output randomness [that] is so
  large" that laid strategic plans can fail to pan out; the reviewer wanted fewer
  cards or more deck-churn tools, confidence: high
- betrayal-legacy (src-005): card draws can leave a player "unable to even meaningfully participate" despite reasonable play, and dice are "the most dickheaded dice", confidence: high
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): extreme damage chains and upgrade-reveal luck are friction even though reviewers still value the resulting tension, confidence: medium
- cthulhu-death-may-die (src-003): "you can get completely ruined by dice and card randomness in this game," wanting a mitigation lever, confidence: medium
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): a first-turn exposure/instant-death result "can knock the wind out of a player and group," interesting only "for the right players", confidence: medium
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): exposure's "random nature can be slightly unfair", confidence: medium
- dune-imperium (src-008): hidden combat inputs (Intrigue cards, troop commitments)
  are praised for making decisions "hard, impactful, and constant" because no one is
  ever fully sure of an opponent's combat strength, confidence: medium
- elder-sign (src-001, src-002): task resolution is fully dice-gated with limited deterministic recourse beyond consumable rerolls, reading as "glorified Yahtzee with custom dice," confidence: high
- elder-sign (src-004): "a few bad rolls... can cause a failure while simultaneously using up all of one's resources," so a bad sequence removes the means to recover from it, confidence: high
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-007): the slipstream/adrenaline catch-up tool is
  called unpredictable and something that "doesn't feel earned or satisfying,"
  read as imposed variance rather than skill, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): randomized enemy sets and dice-driven encounter
  adjustment are named as explicit Pros contributing to replay value,
  confidence: high
- horrified (src-002): item bag and monster deck create uncertainty, but each selected monster supplies a bounded, legible objective puzzle, making the randomness a "productive but visible pressure point", confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-006, src-007, src-009): high-variance systems should be taught as "narrative risk" rather than deterministic tactical control, since the same chaos praised for stories is criticized for domino outcomes and overwhelming randomness, confidence: high
- nemesis (src-007, src-008, src-009): variable objectives and randomized ship layout support replayability, confidence: high
- nemesis-lockdown (src-009): random catastrophic outcomes can "torpedo games despite strategy," obscuring meaningful choices, confidence: medium
- oathsworn-into-the-deepwood (src-005): letting players choose between dice-roll and
  card-draw combat resolution is called an "impressively thoughtful twist" that gives
  risk-tolerant vs. risk-averse players a mitigation choice, confidence: medium
- spirit-island (src-005): the base game's thin fear/blight card pool "repeat[s]
  often," making rounds predictable enough to solve in advance — a variance-too-low
  complaint that is the mirror image of the more common variance-too-high friction,
  confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): the reviewer flags bag-pull variance as a
  strong taste filter, warning that luck-averse players should try the game before
  buying it, confidence: high
- war-of-the-ring (src-002): input vs. output randomness distinguished; hunt draws, combat rolls, and card draws "can frustrate players who prefer deterministic strategy," confidence: medium

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): deterministic no-shuffle deck ordering converts randomness
  into a planning puzzle — the reviewer calls it one of the best cooperative
  deckbuilders specifically because "you never shuffle."
- dune-imperium (src-008): hidden combat strength is praised for producing dramatic,
  legible uncertainty rather than arbitrary noise ("never 100% sure how much combat
  power someone has").
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): dice-driven quest/enemy variability is credited as a
  Pro for replay value, not a complaint.
- oathsworn-into-the-deepwood (src-005): offering dice vs. card resolution as a
  player choice is singled out as a thoughtful risk-mitigation mechanism.
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-006): the rat-tail catch-up rule is praised for
  keeping players who busted badly competitive, softening the sting of a bad bag pull.
- horrified (src-002): per-monster objective design keeps the bag/deck's uncertainty bounded and legible rather than open-ended, the clearest "randomness is a feature, not noise" example added this pass.
- nemesis (src-007, src-008, src-009): variable objectives and randomized ship layout are credited with supporting replayability, distinct from the same game's criticized outcome-determining randomness below.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008, src-009): an oversized card deck produces variance large enough
  that reviewers say players "can't really plan ahead" and end up "waiting for cards
  to come that never do."
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005, src-006, src-007): the adrenaline/slipstream
  catch-up mechanics are repeatedly flagged as feeling "too forgiving or artificial"
  and unearned rather than skill-driven.
- spirit-island (src-005, src-009): the base box under-supplies fear/blight card
  variety, making late rounds solvable in advance, while the true-solo mode swings
  the other way and is called explicitly "swingy."
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): the reviewer's blunt warning — don't buy
  sight-unseen "if you don't like luck" — shows bag-pull variance as a hard filter
  on the audience, not just a texture note.
- elder-sign (src-001, src-002, src-004): fully dice-gated task resolution with limited deterministic recourse, and bad-roll sequences that remove the resources needed to recover, make this the corpus's starkest "randomness overrides skill" case.
- betrayal-legacy (src-005): card draws and "dickheaded" dice can leave a player unable to meaningfully participate despite sound play — variance overriding agency, not just outcome.
- cthulhu-death-may-die (src-003): dice and card randomness can "completely ruin" a well-played game with no named mitigation lever.
- nemesis (src-006, src-007, src-009): the same high-variance systems praised for narrative richness are criticized for domino-like, overwhelming outcomes when they decide who wins — randomness-as-story and randomness-as-skill-override are in direct tension within one game.
- nemesis-lockdown (src-009): random catastrophic outcomes can "torpedo games despite strategy," the sharpest version of the Nemesis-family tension.
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005) and dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): exposure's randomized, sometimes instant-death outcome is explicitly called "slightly unfair" and suited only to "the right players."
- war-of-the-ring (src-002): hunt draws, combat rolls, and card draws are named as frustrating specifically for players who prefer deterministic strategy — the same input/output-randomness distinction Aeon's End resolves by removing output randomness entirely.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): damage-chain and upgrade-reveal luck are friction even where reviewers still value the resulting tension — a milder version of the Nemesis-family split.

## Coverage gaps

Two listed games carried the `randomness` label but didn't yield citable
variance-specific evidence: legendary-encounters-an-alien-deck-building-game's
sourced complaints are about hive-deck *timing* and per-player-count *difficulty*
rather than variance itself, and kingdom-death-monster's only randomness claim is
explicitly flagged by its own doc as an unconfirmed inference (status:
needs_followup). Several of this pass's new citations (betrayal-legacy,
bloodborne-the-card-game, dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game,
dead-of-winter-the-long-night, nemesis-lockdown) also come from `needs_followup`
reception docs, which is why this doc's status drops to `draft` this pass — a
librarian/audit pass clearing that backlog would let it return to `verified`.
