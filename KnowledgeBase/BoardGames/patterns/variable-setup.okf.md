## Summary

Across the corpus, variable/modular setup earns its replayability reputation only
when the pool of variation is large enough to feel fresh; a small or fixed
sub-pool (few fear/blight cards, no modules yet unlocked, mismatched module
sets) turns the same mechanic into a source of predictability or imbalance
complaints instead. The failure mode is rarely "randomized setup is bad" —
it is "there wasn't enough variety in the setup pool," or "the variable
pieces weren't curated correctly." Heavier euros now supply the strongest
"where it works" evidence: Gaia Project and Terra Mystica both credit large
combinatorial setup spaces (board, faction, technology, scoring) directly
with replayability, and Nemesis credits randomized ship layout and variable
objectives the same way. Evidence is still thin per game (one or two citable
claims each).

## Evidence by game

- dominion (src-002, src-003): many Kingdom-card choices let new players "choose how much strategy to apply," confidence: medium
- gaia-project (src-003, src-004): variable board, faction, technology, and final-scoring combinations are central to replayability, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): Roll to Review lists as a con that
  "Early games without modules may feel overly simplistic for seasoned
  players" — the base race alone under-delivers until the optional,
  variable championship modules are added, confidence: medium
- horrified (src-002): the selected-monster system supplies a bounded, legible objective puzzle per setup, making its randomness a "productive but visible pressure point", confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-007, src-008, src-009): variable objectives and randomized ship layout support replayability, confidence: high
- spirit-island (src-005): Gideon's Gaming notes the core box's fear and
  blight decks are small enough to repeat ("low number of fear cards means
  they repeat often"; "only comes with two blight cards"), which the same
  review ties to late-game predictability ("can potentially solve the game
  many rounds in advance"); the same reviewer separately praises the game
  as having "a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value" from its
  modular board and spirit combinations, confidence: high
- terra-mystica (src-003, src-004): reviewers praise 14-faction variety and fully interconnected/deterministic economic systems, confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-004, src-006): The Board Game Family
  explicitly credits variable setup for replayability ("Every game will be
  different"), while Risky Bag Building warns that mismatched
  ingredient-book sets chosen at setup can leave the game "wildly
  unbalanced" if not curated together, confidence: medium (lowest of the
  two cited claims)

## Where it works

- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-004): variable module/ingredient setup is
  named directly as the reason "every game will be different," driving
  family-friendly replayability.
- spirit-island (src-005): modular board and asymmetric spirit setup are
  credited with giving the game "a ton of variety, player choice, and
  replay value."
- gaia-project (src-003, src-004) and terra-mystica (src-003, src-004): both heavy euros credit large combinatorial setup spaces (board, faction, technology, scoring) directly with replayability — the clearest "variety sustains depth" evidence in this pattern.
- nemesis (src-007, src-008, src-009): randomized ship layout and variable objectives are credited with supporting replayability.
- horrified (src-002) and dominion (src-002, src-003): both show variable setup doing double duty as an onboarding lever — a bounded per-monster puzzle, or a scalable Kingdom-card ambition level — rather than pure variety for its own sake.

## Where it fails

- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): without the optional variable modules
  in play, early races can feel "overly simplistic for seasoned players" —
  the base variable-setup pool alone isn't enough to sustain depth.
- spirit-island (src-005): the base game's fear and blight decks are too
  small a variation pool, so games "repeat often" and can become
  predictable in late rounds.
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-006): if players don't curate which
  ingredient-book modules go together at setup, the resulting mix can be
  "wildly unbalanced."

## Coverage gaps

Ark Nova and Root both carry the variable-setup tag, but their reviewed
sources spoke to in-play card-draw randomness (Ark Nova's 212-card zoo
deck) and faction/expansion depth (Root's low-player-count faction mix)
rather than setup-condition variability itself, so neither yielded usable
evidence here. The "where it works" side is now substantially stronger
(Gaia Project, Terra Mystica, Nemesis) than the "where it fails" side, which
still rests on the original three thin-pool complaints — a good WISHLIST
target would be a heavy euro whose reviewers specifically criticize too
*small* a variable-setup combinatorial space, to balance the evidence the
way Spirit Island and Heat already do for lighter games.
