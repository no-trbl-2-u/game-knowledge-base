## Summary

Component-clarity friction shows up two ways across this sample: physical legibility (icon systems, reference cards, board/graphic design) and physical handling (chip durability, table footprint, tracking overhead). Heavier euros and app-adjacent adaptations (Ark Nova, Mage Knight, Slay the Spire) draw complaints that icon-heavy systems demand repeated rulebook/reference lookups rather than resolving from the table state. Lighter, family-weight games (Quacks) draw complaints about physical wear on handled components rather than legibility. Betrayal Legacy and Dark Pact add a third flavor each: physical trackers that fail (clips that slip and damage cards) and permission/resource state that is hard to audit mid-turn without replaying it verbally. The one clean praised-design example (Quacks' status icons) suggests small in-line reminder icons are an effective, low-cost fix for memory load — a much narrower intervention than the "ruthless visual hierarchy" heavy games seem to need.

## Evidence by game

- ark-nova (src-009): Tabletop Gaming's review concludes "component and design irks" keep the game from being the best it could be, using the phrase "veer towards ugly duckling," confidence: medium
- betrayal-legacy (src-005): Shut Up & Sit Down reports the game's tracker clips "will inevitably slip around" and can damage Family cards, a physical-component durability complaint (source doc status: needs_followup), confidence: high
- dark-pact (src-007, src-008, src-010): reviews describe administrative pressure from tracking Action permissions, Gold/Insight resources, and numeric multipliers during long turns, supporting a better-if call to visually separate those three categories so a turn can be audited without replaying it verbally, confidence: medium
- dune-imperium (src-006): a BGG file-page commenter called the square rulebook/reference-sheet format hard to hold and read one-handed and "awkward to use and find space for at the table," confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-006): Co-op Board Games' review lists "Player board iconography requires learning" as a friction point for new players, confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-012): BGG Ultimate Edition rating comments describe "rules checking is 30% of playing time" and "really fiddly rules," tied to the game's site/enemy-effect iconography needing multiple lookups, confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-005, src-008): production and components are praised, but reviewers note the room/card information demands shared reference support and can be hard to inspect across a large table, confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-007, src-010): Rolling In The Meep's dislikes list cites fiddly tracking and "many cubes/effects," and Miniature Market retail snippets call setup "a little lengthy" and "a bit heavy," confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): a reviewer reports "beginning to notice wear on the chips" after just five plays and calls the plastic chips "a bit expensive" to replace, confidence: high

## Where it works

- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-004): The Board Game Family's review praises the game's "icons that help remind players" of ingredient effects, crediting them alongside variable setup for the game's family-friendly accessibility, confidence: high

No other game in this sample produced a review quote that explicitly praises a component-clarity design choice (icon system, board legibility, or reference-card design) as working well; the remaining evidence in this corpus run is friction-only.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-009): "component and design irks" are cited as the reason the game falls short of being the best it could be, confidence: medium
- dune-imperium (src-006): the rulebook/reference-sheet physical format is "bad for using and reading it," awkward to hold and to fit at the table, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-006): player-board iconography "requires learning," slowing onboarding for new players, confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-012): fiddly rules push rules-checking to roughly 30% of playing time, implicating unclear iconography/reference design, confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-007): fiddly cube/effect tracking and a "massive box/table footprint" are named dislikes, confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): repeated handling wears visible marks into the chips within a handful of plays, confidence: high
- betrayal-legacy (src-005): tracker clips slip and can damage Family cards, a physical durability failure distinct from an icon/legibility problem, confidence: high
- dark-pact (src-007, src-008, src-010): Action permissions, resources, and multiplier state are hard to audit mid-turn without visual separation, prompting reviewers to describe long turns as administratively heavy, confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-005, src-008): a large table and dense room/card information mean a rulebook plus two quick-reference cards are needed just to keep the state legible, confidence: medium

## Coverage gaps

Every citable claim here comes from secondary reviews, not from direct component/graphic-design analysis, and only one game (Quacks) offers a genuine praised-design counterexample — the corpus is thin on the "works well" side of this label. betrayal-legacy's contribution comes from a `needs_followup` reception doc, which is why this pattern's `status` stays `draft` rather than `verified`. A game whose reviewers specifically credit iconography or a reference-card redesign for resolving prior complaints (e.g., a revised/second edition) would strengthen the "where it works" side considerably.
