## Summary

Component-clarity friction shows up two ways across this sample: physical legibility (icon systems, reference cards, board/graphic design, miniature/map distinguishability) and physical handling (chip/clip durability, table footprint, tracking overhead, miniature storage). Heavier euros and app-adjacent adaptations (Ark Nova, Mage Knight, Slay the Spire) draw complaints that icon-heavy systems demand repeated rulebook/reference lookups rather than resolving from the table state. Large-footprint campaign and war games (War of the Ring, Nemesis, Cthulhu: Death May Die) add a distinct miniature-legibility failure: similar-looking pieces, crowded boards, and insufficient tile space for the miniature count the box itself ships with. Lighter, family-weight games (Quacks) draw complaints about physical wear on handled components rather than legibility. The one clean praised-design example (Quacks' status icons) suggests small in-line reminder icons are an effective, low-cost fix for memory load — a much narrower intervention than the "ruthless visual hierarchy" heavy games seem to need.

- betrayal-legacy (src-005): tracker clips "will inevitably slip around" and can damage Family cards, confidence: medium
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-006): Meeple Mountain criticizes the drab visual palette, hurting quick reading in the simultaneous, timing-sensitive environment, confidence: medium
- cthulhu-death-may-die (src-003): "you will run out of room for miniatures on some of the tiles," wanting a documented stacking/substitution convention, confidence: medium
- dune-imperium (src-006): a BGG file-page commenter called the square rulebook/reference-sheet format hard to hold and read one-handed and "awkward to use and find space for at the table," confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-006): Co-op Board Games' review lists "Player board iconography requires learning" as a friction point for new players, confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-012): BGG Ultimate Edition rating comments describe "rules checking is 30% of playing time" and "really fiddly rules," tied to the game's site/enemy-effect iconography needing multiple lookups, confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-005, src-008): component spectacle is praised, but room/card information demands shared reference support and is hard to inspect across the large table footprint, confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-007, src-010): Rolling In The Meep's dislikes list cites fiddly tracking and "many cubes/effects," and Miniature Market retail snippets call setup "a little lengthy" and "a bit heavy," confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): a reviewer reports "beginning to notice wear on the chips" after just five plays and calls the plastic chips "a bit expensive" to replace, confidence: high
- war-of-the-ring (src-002, src-008): crowded map areas, covered location names, and similar-looking miniatures make factions/nations hard to distinguish, confidence: high

## Where it works

- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-004): The Board Game Family's review praises the game's "icons that help remind players" of ingredient effects, crediting them alongside variable setup for the game's family-friendly accessibility, confidence: high
- nemesis (src-005, src-008): component spectacle itself draws genuine reviewer praise — the complaint is about inspecting shared information at the table, not about the components' visual/thematic design.

No other game in this sample produced a review quote that explicitly praises a component-clarity design choice (icon system, board legibility, or reference-card design) as working well; the remaining evidence in this corpus run is friction-only.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-009): "component and design irks" are cited as the reason the game falls short of being the best it could be, confidence: medium
- dune-imperium (src-006): the rulebook/reference-sheet physical format is "bad for using and reading it," awkward to hold and to fit at the table, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-006): player-board iconography "requires learning," slowing onboarding for new players, confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-012): fiddly rules push rules-checking to roughly 30% of playing time, implicating unclear iconography/reference design, confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-007): fiddly cube/effect tracking and a "massive box/table footprint" are named dislikes, confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): repeated handling wears visible marks into the chips within a handful of plays, confidence: high
- betrayal-legacy (src-005): slipping tracker clips risk physical damage to permanent campaign components, a sharper failure mode than ordinary wear since the damaged cards are irreplaceable mid-campaign.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-006): a drab visual palette slows the quick reads that simultaneous, timing-sensitive play depends on.
- cthulhu-death-may-die (src-003): the box's own miniature count can outrun the board's printed tile space, a production/board-design mismatch rather than a pure iconography problem.
- war-of-the-ring (src-002, src-008): crowded maps and similar miniatures make the core factional distinction — whose piece is whose — hard to read at a glance, undercutting a wargame's most basic legibility need.
- nemesis (src-005, src-008): shared room/card information is hard to inspect across a large table even when the components themselves are well-produced.

## Coverage gaps

The "where it works" side is still thin (two partial examples against eleven friction citations), but the failure side now shows a clear split between icon/reference-design problems (heavier euros) and miniature/map-legibility problems at scale (war and horror-campaign games). A game whose reviewers specifically credit iconography, a reference-card redesign, or a miniature/map legibility pass for resolving prior complaints (e.g., a revised/second edition) would strengthen the "where it works" side considerably.
