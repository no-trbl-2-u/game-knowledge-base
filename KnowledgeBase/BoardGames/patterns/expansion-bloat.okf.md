## Summary

Evidence remains narrow but now spans four games and shows more than one
shape of expansion-bloat friction. Arkham Horror: The Card Game and Root
illustrate "completeness pressure": the base game is judged a fine design
on its own, but the surrounding product line (reprints, more character
copies, more factions) becomes an implicit requirement to reach the
"ideal" experience, and that pressure — not raw content volume — is what
draws criticism. Dominion supplies a related but distinct case: "expansion
fatigue" is named only as a minor flaw, folded into a broader, ever-growing
rules ecosystem large enough that the sprawl itself registers as friction
even without a single expansion being singled out. Dead of Winter: The Long
Night cuts differently on quality — its Raxxon expansion is called "a very
tough but enjoyable addition," so individual expansion content can land
well — but the same reviewer is unsure whether it's "worthwhile" to buy if
you already own the base game, an economic/redundancy doubt distinct from
either completeness pressure or fatigue. Together these four games suggest
expansion-bloat friction takes at least three forms: pressure to complete a
growing product line, fatigue from an ever-expanding ruleset, and doubt
about per-purchase value even when the expansion content itself is good.

## Evidence by game

- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-005): Board Game Quest frames the revised
  core set as a response to reprint scarcity that "strained" the product
  line's ability to bring in new players, i.e. product-line sprawl and
  availability became an onboarding tax rather than a content benefit,
  confidence: medium
- root (src-006): Gideon's Gaming notes expansion factions create a
  perceived-completeness pressure — "having them all is the ideal
  experience" — and that "the Law of Root booklet itself even heightens the
  feeling of incompleteness," confidence: medium
- dominion (src-003, src-007): "Expansion fatigue" is named as a minor flaw
  amid a broader, ever-growing rules ecosystem, confidence: medium
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): The Raxxon expansion is "a very
  tough but enjoyable addition," confidence: high
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): The reviewer is "not sure...
  worthwhile purchase if you've already got the original game," confidence: medium

## Where it works

- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): The Raxxon expansion itself is
  praised as "a very tough but enjoyable addition" — evidence that expansion
  content can succeed on its own design merits even where its purchase-value
  case is questioned.

No other game in this set produced praised-design evidence tied specifically
to an expansion or the expansion product line as a whole.

## Where it fails

- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-005): reprint scarcity and product-line
  strain hampered new-player entry — "Due to a lack of reprints and
  scarcity, the system was strained by continuing forward while losing the
  ability to bring in new players."
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-007): base-game content is explicitly
  capped without buying more product — "If you wish to play with more than
  two characters, you will need another copy of the base game."
- root (src-006): the official rules booklet itself is cited as amplifying
  the sense that the base game alone is incomplete, pushing players toward
  the full expansion set to get the "ideal" experience.
- dominion (src-003, src-007): the game's continually expanding rules
  ecosystem is enough to produce reviewer-named "expansion fatigue," even
  when framed as a minor flaw rather than a dealbreaker.
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): despite praising the Raxxon
  expansion's content, the same reviewer doubts it is a "worthwhile purchase"
  for owners of the original game — a value/redundancy complaint separate
  from content quality.

## Coverage gaps

Current support for this label comes from reviewer commentary rather than
direct player-count survey data. The sample now includes a deck-building
game with long-running incremental releases (Dominion) and a legacy-adjacent
campaign game with a single large expansion (Dead of Winter: The Long
Night), which broadens the pattern beyond campaign/asymmetric-board designs
but still leaves it thin per-game. Games with large, ongoing expansion
catalogs beyond Dominion (other long-running LCGs, app-supported games with
frequent content drops) would further test whether "completeness pressure,"
"fatigue," and "per-purchase value doubt" are the complete set of failure
shapes or whether additional ones emerge at greater scale.
