## Summary

Across the corpus, players judge deck/bag/pool-building systems less on whether
randomness exists and more on whether they can *see* it coming. Reviewers
praise designs that give players legible control over pool composition —
deterministic no-shuffle sequencing, variable per-game bag setup, escalating
threat decks with a readable clock — because those turn the pool into a
planning surface. Friction shows up wherever the pool feels stacked against
the player without warning: weak starter card pools before the system opens
up, escalating threat decks whose spawn timing collides with objectives, and
literal token wear undermining trust in the physical bag mechanism. Evidence
is real but thin per game (one or two citable claims each), and one tagged
game (Hoplomachus: Remastered) has no reviewer-level evidence on its chip-pull
system at all yet.

## Evidence by game

- aeons-end (src-004): The no-shuffle deck rewards deliberate pool sequencing and punishes bloat directly — "If you bloat your deck even a bit, you will pay for it," and a loss reads as "a bad choice," not a bad draw. Confidence: high
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006): Preconstructed starter card pools are criticized as "poor, unfocused," so new players lose with "few options" before the wider deck-construction system opens up. Confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-006): Accepting Heat tokens into a player's personal deck — diluting future draws for present speed — is called out as producing "meaningful decisions." Confidence: medium
- legendary-encounters-an-alien-deck-building-game (src-003): The hidden hive deck (an escalating threat pool) creates timing ambiguity when objective completion and hive spawning overlap, and grows "brutally hard" as more drones are added per player. Confidence: medium
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Bag-draw variance is central enough to gate the purchase recommendation itself — reviewer would not "recommend buying this without playing it or seeing it played first if you don't like luck." Confidence: high

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): Meeple Mountain calls it "one of the best cooperative deck-builders" because the no-shuffle rule means players "never shuffle" and "set combos in motion through meticulous planning" — pool order becomes a skill expression, not a luck roll.
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006): There Will Be Games states it is "the best deck construction/deck building game I have played," crediting the campaign-long pool-construction arc.
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-004): The Board Game Family praises variable per-game bag composition — "Every game will be different" — as core to family replayability.
- legendary-encounters-an-alien-deck-building-game (src-003): Reviewer credits "the stress of the hive deck" and "panic when an event or hazard gets triggered" as what makes the cooperative deckbuilding economy feel dangerous rather than solitaire.

## Where it fails

- aeons-end (src-005): Fixed market pool is called limited — "La variedad del mercado es algo limitada."
- arkham-horror-the-card-game (src-006): Same preconstructed-pool weakness cuts both ways — "losing with bad decks with few options sucks" for new players before the card pool expands.
- legendary-encounters-an-alien-deck-building-game (src-003): Hive-deck (threat-pool) spawn timing versus objective completion is flagged as an unresolved friction point, and difficulty scaling by player count is not transparent at setup.
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): The physical bag-and-chip system itself wears down — "After even just 5 games I was beginning to notice wear on the chips" — undermining the tactile trust the mechanic depends on, alongside the raw luck-tolerance gate noted above.
