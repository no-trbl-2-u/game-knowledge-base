## Summary

Across these games, "player interaction" friction clusters into three shapes: (1) euro-style games bolt on confrontation as a discrete subsystem (attack cards, denial effects) that reviewers read as tacked-on rather than integrated; (2) cooperative/simultaneous games get their interaction "for free" from shared mechanisms (aid tokens, enemy handoffs, free-order actions), but that same lack of turn-by-turn structure opens quarterbacking or cheating-adjacent private-information risk; and (3) semi-cooperative/hidden-objective games make suspicion itself the interaction — hidden goals and survival pressure keep the group nominally cooperating even when one player's agenda is hostile (Nemesis), while a design that promises cooperation but delivers little of it reads as a letdown (Onirim's two-player mode, "essentially two-player solitaire"). Distinct opposing roles (Descent 2E's Overlord, Imperial Assault's Imperial player) are praised as a structural way to guarantee interaction rather than leaving it optional. The lesson: interaction works best when it is a structural byproduct of shared stakes or explicit asymmetric roles rather than an add-on module, and simultaneous/private mechanisms need an explicit audit or commitment ritual to stay trustworthy.

## Evidence by game

- ark-nova (src-007): Reviewer says the game's attack cards "seem like a tacked on solution for more player interaction" in an otherwise largely parallel euro; confidence: high
- descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition (src-001, src-003): BGG and publisher descriptions both foreground the Overlord-versus-heroes role split as the campaign's defining interaction structure, though no reviewer commentary on interaction quality specifically (e.g. table dynamics between the Overlord player and heroes) was found beyond the role-split framing itself; confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Reviewer flags open information in the co-op as a quarterbacking/alpha-player risk; confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): Reviews describe hidden objectives creating suspicion while survival pressure preserves a reason to cooperate rather than reveal sabotage immediately — direct attacks are prohibited but indirect harm is legal, so cooperation often remains self-preserving even for hostile agendas; confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): Reviewer describes the two-player cooperative mode as offering little real cooperation, closer to "two-player solitaire," rating solo play more highly than the co-op mode; confidence: high
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): Reviewer frames the game around giving one player a distinct opposing (Imperial) role against 1-4 Rebel heroes, a structural guarantee of direct player interaction rather than a bolted-on module; confidence: high
- mage-knight (src-011): Fan critique argues the competitive mode's interaction is denial-driven — other players taking dice/actions/spells/units or killing targets injects unwanted luck — and calls co-op "not cooperative enough," i.e. "multiplayer solitaire"; confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): Reviewer praises the rulebook's free-order rule (players may play cards/use potions/activate abilities in any order) as the mechanism enabling tactical co-op collaboration, but flags that this strength needs explicit teaching; confidence: medium
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Reviewer notes the simultaneous, largely private bag-drawing phase creates "many opportunities to cheat" in what is otherwise "largely solitaire" play; confidence: medium

## Where it works

- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Aid tokens and enemy handoffs give players concrete, repeated reasons to coordinate rather than merely play in parallel.
- ark-nova (src-004): The official FAQ lets groups that dislike interactive Poison/Pilfering-style abilities swap in alternate solo-game effects, formalizing interaction as an optional mode instead of a fixed tax on every table.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): The rulebook's explicit any-order action rule is called out as the feature that makes cooperative play read as genuine collaboration rather than turn-taking.
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): Hidden objectives and a prohibition on direct attacks are praised as the mechanism that keeps a semi-cooperative table tense without collapsing into open conflict.
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): A structurally guaranteed opposing role (the Imperial player) is treated as a design strength, ensuring interaction is built into every session rather than optional.
- descent-journeys-in-the-dark-second-edition (src-001, src-003): The Overlord-versus-heroes split is likewise foregrounded as the campaign's core structural interaction, though without a reviewer quote specifically praising how that interaction plays out at the table.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-007): Attack/interaction cards feel like a bolted-on answer to "the game needs more interaction," not a system-native one.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Fully open information invites one player to dictate the group's choices.
- mage-knight (src-011): Competitive-mode interaction is experienced as denial and added variance from other players' actions rather than meaningful engagement; the co-op mode is described as "multiplayer solitaire."
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Private simultaneous resolution trades away auditability — the same design that avoids downtime also removes the ability to verify others aren't cheating.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): The two-player cooperative mode is criticized for offering too little real cooperation, functioning closer to two-player solitaire than a joint effort.

## Coverage gaps

The original five contributing claims came from cooperative or largely-parallel euro designs where interaction is either absent-by-default or added as a discrete module. Nemesis, Onirim, Descent 2E, and Star Wars: Imperial Assault broaden this to semi-cooperative hidden-objective design and structurally-guaranteed asymmetric-role interaction, but the corpus still lacks a game whose central mechanic is negotiation, trading, or direct competitive conflict outside a co-op/semi-co-op frame — a title like that would test whether integrated (rather than bolted-on) interaction produces different reviewer language than the semi-cooperative and cooperative designs surveyed here.
