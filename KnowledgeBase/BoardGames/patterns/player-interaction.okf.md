## Summary

Across the current evidence, "player interaction" friction clusters into two shapes: (1) euro-style games bolt on confrontation as a discrete subsystem (attack cards, denial effects) that reviewers read as tacked-on rather than integrated; (2) cooperative/simultaneous games get their interaction "for free" from shared mechanisms (aid tokens, enemy handoffs, free-order actions), but that same lack of turn-by-turn structure opens quarterbacking or cheating-adjacent private-information risk. The lesson: interaction works best when it is a structural byproduct of shared stakes (co-op handoffs, shared defeat conditions) rather than an add-on module, and simultaneous/private mechanisms need an explicit audit or commitment ritual to stay trustworthy. New evidence adds a third praised shape: *hidden-goal* tension (Nemesis's private objectives, Shadows over Camelot's traitor accusation, The Crew's token-limited communication) consistently reads as the source of engagement reviewers single out by name, while games that are cooperative but low on structural interaction (Onirim's "essentially two-player solitaire," Dominion's "lower player interaction" as a named minor flaw) draw the opposite complaint even when nothing else about the design is criticized.

## Evidence by game

- ark-nova (src-007): Reviewer says the game's attack cards "seem like a tacked on solution for more player interaction" in an otherwise largely parallel euro; confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Reviewer flags open information in the co-op as a quarterbacking/alpha-player risk; confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-011): Fan critique argues the competitive mode's interaction is denial-driven — other players taking dice/actions/spells/units or killing targets injects unwanted luck — and calls co-op "not cooperative enough," i.e. "multiplayer solitaire"; confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): Reviewer praises the rulebook's free-order rule (players may play cards/use potions/activate abilities in any order) as the mechanism enabling tactical co-op collaboration, but flags that this strength needs explicit teaching; confidence: medium
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Reviewer notes the simultaneous, largely private bag-drawing phase creates "many opportunities to cheat" in what is otherwise "largely solitaire" play; confidence: medium
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): Ars praises bluffing and betrayal between players but warns the take-that play will not suit groups uncomfortable attacking friends' plans; confidence: high
- dominion (src-003): Board Game Quest's Final Thoughts lists "lower player interaction" among the game's minor flaws even while rating it 4.5 stars overall; confidence: high
- food-chain-magnate (src-004): the independent review praises the game's telegraphed threats, saying opponent moves provide legible clues about the market, but separately calls the resulting competition "brutally punishing at times"; confidence: high
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): reviews repeatedly identify two private hidden objectives — not a conventional revealed-traitor structure — as the actual tension source, contrasting public cooperation with private agenda; confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the reviewer says there is little cooperation in the two-player mode, characterizing it as "essentially two-player solitaire"; confidence: medium
- scythe (src-003): the reviewer says the game presents as a combat title but combat is rare during a typical session, muting the direct-conflict interaction the box markets; confidence: high
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): traitor uncertainty is identified as the thing driving interaction, with reviewers praising the accusation mechanism by name; confidence: high
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-004): the review frames the entire design around one Imperial player against 1–4 Rebel heroes as a strength, not friction; confidence: high
- terra-mystica (src-003, src-004): adjacent opponents are praised for making land competitive and power-rich, while the same reviews separately list "lower amount of player interaction" as a Miss; confidence: high
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): the manual's one-token-per-mission communication limit is credited by a reviewer with creating tension rather than frustration; confidence: high
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): four-player play is reported to bog down as fighters muddy into and around each other on the shared grid; confidence: medium

## Where it works

- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Aid tokens and enemy handoffs give players concrete, repeated reasons to coordinate rather than merely play in parallel.
- ark-nova (src-004): The official FAQ lets groups that dislike interactive Poison/Pilfering-style abilities swap in alternate solo-game effects, formalizing interaction as an optional mode instead of a fixed tax on every table.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): The rulebook's explicit any-order action rule is called out as the feature that makes cooperative play read as genuine collaboration rather than turn-taking.
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): private hidden objectives are named directly as the source of tension across three independent reviews, the strongest "named by multiple sources" praise in this pattern.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): traitor-accusation uncertainty is singled out by name as what makes the social layer work.
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): a strict one-token-per-mission communication limit is credited with creating tension rather than being read as a restriction.
- food-chain-magnate (src-004): telegraphed opponent moves give every player legible market signals, making the game's harsh competition feel earned rather than arbitrary.
- terra-mystica (src-003): competing for adjacent land is praised as a direct, legible interaction lever even in an otherwise parallel-development euro.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-007): Attack/interaction cards feel like a bolted-on answer to "the game needs more interaction," not a system-native one.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Fully open information invites one player to dictate the group's choices.
- mage-knight (src-011): Competitive-mode interaction is experienced as denial and added variance from other players' actions rather than meaningful engagement; the co-op mode is described as "multiplayer solitaire."
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Private simultaneous resolution trades away auditability — the same design that avoids downtime also removes the ability to verify others aren't cheating.
- dominion (src-003): a reviewer names "lower player interaction" as a minor flaw in an otherwise highly-rated (4.5-star) deckbuilder, showing the complaint can persist even when nothing else about a design draws criticism.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): two-player cooperative mode is described as "essentially two-player solitaire," the sharpest "interaction is absent" complaint in this pattern.
- scythe (src-003): combat is marketed as central but reported rare in actual sessions, a mismatch between the interaction the box promises and what the table experiences.
- terra-mystica (src-003): the same adjacency mechanic praised as a legible interaction lever is also cited in a Misses list as evidence of overall "lower amount of player interaction."
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): four-player games are reported to bog down as fighters crowd each other on the shared grid, degrading the interaction the asymmetric-duel design is built around.

## Coverage gaps

The contributing claims increasingly do include games with negotiation, hidden-goal tension, or direct conflict as a core loop (Nemesis, Shadows over Camelot, Bloodborne), narrowing last pass's gap. What remains thin is a genuine trading/negotiation-economy game (e.g. one built around explicit player-to-player deals) — Nemesis's negotiation mechanic tag didn't yield a negotiation-specific claim here, so integrated trading interaction is still untested in this pattern.
