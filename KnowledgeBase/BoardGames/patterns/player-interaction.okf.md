## Summary

Across the current evidence, "player interaction" friction clusters into three shapes: (1) euro-style games bolt on confrontation as a discrete subsystem (attack cards, denial effects) that reviewers read as tacked-on rather than integrated; (2) cooperative/simultaneous games get their interaction "for free" from shared mechanisms (aid tokens, enemy handoffs, free-order actions), but that same lack of turn-by-turn structure opens quarterbacking or cheating-adjacent private-information risk; (3) a newly-evidenced cluster of semi-cooperative/hidden-traitor games (Shadows over Camelot, Nemesis) shows the opposite lesson — reviewers name imperfect information and costly, uncertain accusation as the *strongest* fun in the design, not a risk to mitigate. Restricted-communication co-op (The Crew) sits between these: scarcity of communication is explicitly the design's tension, not a bug. The lesson: interaction works best when it is a structural byproduct of shared or divergent stakes (co-op handoffs, shared defeat conditions, hidden individual objectives) rather than an add-on module; simultaneous/fully-open designs need an explicit audit or commitment ritual to stay trustworthy, while hidden-objective designs should protect — not resolve — their uncertainty.

## Evidence by game

- ark-nova (src-007): Reviewer says the game's attack cards "seem like a tacked on solution for more player interaction" in an otherwise largely parallel euro; confidence: high
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Reviewer flags open information in the co-op as a quarterbacking/alpha-player risk; confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-011): Fan critique argues the competitive mode's interaction is denial-driven — other players taking dice/actions/spells/units or killing targets injects unwanted luck — and calls co-op "not cooperative enough," i.e. "multiplayer solitaire"; confidence: medium
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): Reviewer praises the rulebook's free-order rule (players may play cards/use potions/activate abilities in any order) as the mechanism enabling tactical co-op collaboration, but flags that this strength needs explicit teaching; confidence: medium
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Reviewer notes the simultaneous, largely private bag-drawing phase creates "many opportunities to cheat" in what is otherwise "largely solitaire" play; confidence: medium
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): The manual limits normal communication to one token per player per mission, and Board Game Quest identifies that constrained communication as part of the game's tension — any communication aid should preserve the scarcity rather than turn it into unrestricted table talk; confidence: high
- dominion (src-003): Board Game Quest lists lower player interaction as a minor flaw in an otherwise-praised design, supporting one more consequential interaction channel without slowing the game's short turns; confidence: medium
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the reviewer says the two-player mode has "little cooperation" and characterizes it as essentially two-player solitaire, supporting one more consequential shared decision beyond shared resources and communication limits; confidence: medium
- scythe (src-001, src-003): the publisher describes direct conflict without elimination, while the reviewer reports that factions usually build engines and hold territory rather than fight constantly — player-interaction expectations deserve explicit framing so territorial pressure isn't assumed to mean constant combat; confidence: medium
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): Ars Technica praises bluffing and betrayal but warns take-that play won't suit groups uncomfortable with attacking friends' plans, supporting plain onboarding disclosure that promises are nonbinding; confidence: high (source doc status: needs_followup)
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): the reviewer reports Crossroads triggers "only had one or two of the cards come into play" across sessions, a narrow player-interaction gap between the mechanic's premise and its observed table frequency; confidence: medium (source doc status: needs_followup)
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): imperfect communication, uncertain Traitor presence, and costly accusation should be preserved rather than made transparent — both reviewers independently identify suspicion around ordinary moves as the base game's strongest fun; confidence: high (source doc status: needs_followup)
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): objective and semi-cooperative expectations should be calibrated precisely — direct attacks are prohibited, indirect harm is legal, and cooperation often remains self-preserving even for hostile agendas; reviews repeatedly identify hidden goals, not a conventional revealed-traitor structure, as the tension source; confidence: high

## Where it works

- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Aid tokens and enemy handoffs give players concrete, repeated reasons to coordinate rather than merely play in parallel.
- ark-nova (src-004): The official FAQ lets groups that dislike interactive Poison/Pilfering-style abilities swap in alternate solo-game effects, formalizing interaction as an optional mode instead of a fixed tax on every table.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): The rulebook's explicit any-order action rule is called out as the feature that makes cooperative play read as genuine collaboration rather than turn-taking.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): imperfect information and the possibility of a hidden Traitor are named as the base game's strongest fun by two independent reviewers — the first entry in this pattern where withheld information is praised rather than flagged as risk (source doc status: needs_followup).
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): a hidden-objective structure (rather than a revealed-traitor team split) is repeatedly credited as the actual tension source, with cooperation remaining rational even under hostile individual goals.
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): a hard-limited communication token, rather than open table talk, is the design's stated source of tension and is treated as a feature to protect, not a gap to fill.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-007): Attack/interaction cards feel like a bolted-on answer to "the game needs more interaction," not a system-native one.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Fully open information invites one player to dictate the group's choices.
- mage-knight (src-011): Competitive-mode interaction is experienced as denial and added variance from other players' actions rather than meaningful engagement; the co-op mode is described as "multiplayer solitaire."
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Private simultaneous resolution trades away auditability — the same design that avoids downtime also removes the ability to verify others aren't cheating.
- dominion (src-003): lower player interaction is named as one of the review's few flaws in an otherwise strongly-praised short-turn design.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the two-player mode is described as functionally solitaire rather than cooperative, the sharpest "interaction absent" complaint in this set.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): take-that bluffing and betrayal will not suit groups uncomfortable with direct interference, a fit-warning rather than a design flaw (source doc status: needs_followup).
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): a headline social-deduction mechanic (Crossroads) triggered far less often than its billing suggested in the reviewer's sessions (source doc status: needs_followup).

## Coverage gaps

The contributing claims now span cooperative, semi-cooperative/hidden-traitor, and euro designs, resolving the prior gap calling for negotiation or hidden-information-driven interaction evidence — Shadows over Camelot and Nemesis both supply it, though both draw on needs_followup-status reception docs and should be reweighted once those clear review. What remains thin is a game whose central mechanic is direct trading or negotiated resource exchange (as opposed to combat, accusation, or communication scarcity); none of the cited games treat trading as a core loop.
