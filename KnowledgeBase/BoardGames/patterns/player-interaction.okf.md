## Summary

Across the current evidence, "player interaction" friction clusters into three shapes: (1) euro-style games bolt on confrontation as a discrete subsystem (attack cards, denial effects) that reviewers read as tacked-on rather than integrated; (2) cooperative/simultaneous games get their interaction "for free" from shared mechanisms (aid tokens, enemy handoffs, free-order actions), but that same lack of turn-by-turn structure opens quarterbacking or cheating-adjacent private-information risk; and (3) hidden-role/semi-cooperative games (Shadows over Camelot, Nemesis, Bloodborne) get their strongest praised interaction specifically from *imperfect* information — uncertain traitor presence, nonbinding promises, self-preserving hidden agendas — where reviewers explicitly warn that making cooperation fully transparent would remove the game's best fun, not improve it. The lesson: interaction works best when it is a structural byproduct of shared stakes or engineered uncertainty (co-op handoffs, shared defeat conditions, hidden agendas) rather than an add-on module, and simultaneous/private mechanisms need an explicit audit, commitment ritual, or up-front expectation-setting to stay trustworthy rather than merely confusing.

## Evidence by game

- ark-nova (src-007): Reviewer says the game's attack cards "seem like a tacked on solution for more player interaction" in an otherwise largely parallel euro; confidence: high
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): the bluffing/betrayal design means promises are nonbinding and direct interference is intentional — onboarding should state this plainly since "take-that play will not suit groups uncomfortable with attacking friends' plans," confidence: high
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): Crossroads triggers, the game's built-in narrative-interaction channel, "only had one or two of the cards come into play" in the reviewer's sessions, narrowing that channel in practice, confidence: medium
- dominion (src-003): Board Game Quest explicitly lists lower player interaction as a minor flaw in an otherwise praised quick, accessible design, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Reviewer flags open information in the co-op as a quarterbacking/alpha-player risk; confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-011): Fan critique argues the competitive mode's interaction is denial-driven — other players taking dice/actions/spells/units or killing targets injects unwanted luck — and calls co-op "not cooperative enough," i.e. "multiplayer solitaire"; confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): reviews repeatedly identify hidden goals, not a conventional revealed-traitor structure, as the tension source, supporting a call to calibrate semi-coop expectations up front — direct attacks prohibited, indirect harm legal, cooperation often self-preserving even for hostile agendas, confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the two-player mode gives partners little real cooperation beyond shared resources and communication limits, with the reviewer characterizing it as "essentially two-player solitaire," confidence: medium
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): both reviewers independently name suspicion around ordinary moves — imperfect communication, uncertain Traitor presence, costly accusation — as the base game's strongest fun, and warn that making cooperation fully transparent would remove it, confidence: high
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): Reviewer praises the rulebook's free-order rule (players may play cards/use potions/activate abilities in any order) as the mechanism enabling tactical co-op collaboration, but flags that this strength needs explicit teaching; confidence: medium
- star-wars-imperial-assault (src-003, src-004): between-mission administration should preserve narrative continuity without demanding a full recurring group, since group-size concerns and campaign progression are both central to how reviewers discuss the dual-role structure, confidence: medium
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Reviewer notes the simultaneous, largely private bag-drawing phase creates "many opportunities to cheat" in what is otherwise "largely solitaire" play; confidence: medium

## Where it works

- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Aid tokens and enemy handoffs give players concrete, repeated reasons to coordinate rather than merely play in parallel.
- ark-nova (src-004): The official FAQ lets groups that dislike interactive Poison/Pilfering-style abilities swap in alternate solo-game effects, formalizing interaction as an optional mode instead of a fixed tax on every table.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): The rulebook's explicit any-order action rule is called out as the feature that makes cooperative play read as genuine collaboration rather than turn-taking.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): imperfect communication and the ever-present possibility of a hidden Traitor are named as the design's single strongest source of fun — interaction here is the uncertainty itself, not a bolted-on subsystem.
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): hidden, often self-preserving agendas are credited as the actual source of tension across three independent reviews, distinct from and stronger than a simple revealed-traitor mechanic would produce.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): Ars Technica praises the bluffing-and-betrayal core as the thing that makes the game "actually pretty great," provided groups know what they're signing up for.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-007): Attack/interaction cards feel like a bolted-on answer to "the game needs more interaction," not a system-native one.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Fully open information invites one player to dictate the group's choices.
- mage-knight (src-011): Competitive-mode interaction is experienced as denial and added variance from other players' actions rather than meaningful engagement; the co-op mode is described as "multiplayer solitaire."
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Private simultaneous resolution trades away auditability — the same design that avoids downtime also removes the ability to verify others aren't cheating.
- dominion (src-003): a fast, largely parallel euro turn structure leaves interaction as an explicitly named minor flaw rather than a design strength.
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): a designed interaction channel (Crossroads cards) can go almost entirely unused across a real play session, leaving the game closer to parallel play than its design suggests.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): two-player "cooperative" play can read as functionally solitaire when the only shared elements are resources and communication limits.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): the same nonbinding-promise design that produces its best interaction is explicitly flagged as unsuitable for groups uncomfortable with take-that play against friends.

## Coverage gaps

The earlier gap here — a game whose central mechanic is negotiation, trading, or direct player-vs-player conflict — is now substantially addressed: Shadows over Camelot, Nemesis, and Bloodborne: The Card Game all contribute hidden-agenda/betrayal-driven interaction evidence, giving this pattern its first genuine "engineered uncertainty as interaction" cluster distinct from the earlier co-op/euro material. What remains thin is a game where negotiation or trading is the literal core action (as opposed to hidden roles layered onto cooperation) — Root and Nemesis both carry a `negotiation` mechanics tag but neither's cited claims here are about negotiation as a resource-trading loop specifically.
