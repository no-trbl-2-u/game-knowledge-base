## Summary

Across the current evidence, "player interaction" friction clusters into three shapes: (1) euro-style games bolt on confrontation as a discrete subsystem (attack cards, denial effects) that reviewers read as tacked-on rather than integrated, or simply report low interaction as a named flaw without a subsystem to blame (Dominion, Terra Mystica); (2) cooperative/simultaneous games get their interaction "for free" from shared mechanisms (aid tokens, enemy handoffs, free-order actions, scarce communication tokens), but that same lack of turn-by-turn structure opens quarterbacking or cheating-adjacent private-information risk; (3) hidden-role/semi-cooperative games (Nemesis, Shadows over Camelot) get interaction specifically from *suspicion* — players watching each other's ordinary moves for signs of hidden allegiance — which reviewers single out as their strongest fun, distinct from both bolted-on confrontation and cooperative handoffs. The lesson: interaction works best when it is a structural byproduct of shared stakes or hidden information (co-op handoffs, suspicion, scarce communication) rather than an add-on module, and simultaneous/private mechanisms need an explicit audit or commitment ritual to stay trustworthy.

## Evidence by game

- ark-nova (src-007): Reviewer says the game's attack cards "seem like a tacked on solution for more player interaction" in an otherwise largely parallel euro; confidence: high
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): bluffing and betrayal are praised, but take-that play "will not suit groups uncomfortable with attacking friends' plans", confidence: high
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): Crossroads triggers rarely fired; reviewer "only had one or two of the cards come into play", confidence: medium
- dominion (src-003): Board Game Quest "explicitly lists lower player interaction as a minor flaw" while praising quick turns/accessibility, confidence: medium
- food-chain-magnate (src-004): reviewer praises telegraphed threats and visible opponent moves ("clues about the market") as counterplay, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Reviewer flags open information in the co-op as a quarterbacking/alpha-player risk; confidence: medium
- mage-knight (src-011): Fan critique argues the competitive mode's interaction is denial-driven — other players taking dice/actions/spells/units or killing targets injects unwanted luck — and calls co-op "not cooperative enough," i.e. "multiplayer solitaire"; confidence: medium
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009): hidden objectives create suspicion while survival pressure preserves a reason to cooperate rather than reveal sabotage immediately, confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): two-player cooperation is limited, described as close to two-player solitaire, confidence: high
- scythe (src-001, src-003): publisher frames direct conflict without elimination; reviewer reports factions usually build engines and maintain territory rather than fight constantly, confidence: medium
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): both reviewers identify suspicion around ordinary moves, uncertain Traitor presence, and costly accusation as the base game's strongest fun, confidence: high
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): Reviewer praises the rulebook's free-order rule (players may play cards/use potions/activate abilities in any order) as the mechanism enabling tactical co-op collaboration, but flags that this strength needs explicit teaching; confidence: medium
- star-wars-rebellion (src-006): four-player team mode splits leaders between teammates and reduces individual agency; both reviewers treat the game as "really 2" players at heart, confidence: high
- terra-mystica (src-002, src-004): SPACE-BIFF! says counterplay against an ahead player is just "you build in such a way that it blocks them, and that's about it," with no named catch-up action; Board Game Quest's Misses list includes "Lower amount of player interaction," confidence: high
- terra-mystica (src-003): the proximity system creates a meaningful tradeoff between accessing opponents' power and competing for contested land, confidence: high
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): manual limits communication to one token/player/mission; Board Game Quest identifies this constrained communication as part of the game's tension, confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Reviewer notes the simultaneous, largely private bag-drawing phase creates "many opportunities to cheat" in what is otherwise "largely solitaire" play; confidence: medium
- twilight-imperium-fourth-edition (src-003): each strategy card has a secondary effect usable by other players, and the reviewer's first game featured alliances and betrayals, confidence: medium
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): four-player play "bogs down" as fighters muddy into each other on the battlefield, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Aid tokens and enemy handoffs give players concrete, repeated reasons to coordinate rather than merely play in parallel.
- ark-nova (src-004): The official FAQ lets groups that dislike interactive Poison/Pilfering-style abilities swap in alternate solo-game effects, formalizing interaction as an optional mode instead of a fixed tax on every table.
- slay-the-spire-the-board-game (src-008): The rulebook's explicit any-order action rule is called out as the feature that makes cooperative play read as genuine collaboration rather than turn-taking.
- nemesis (src-006, src-008, src-009) and shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): both hidden-role games get their strongest praised fun from suspicion over ordinary moves — interaction that comes from not knowing who to trust, not from a bolted-on confrontation subsystem.
- the-crew (src-002, src-003): deliberately scarce communication tokens are credited with producing tension rather than frustration — restriction itself is the praised design, not a workaround.
- food-chain-magnate (src-004): visible, telegraphed opponent moves let players read and react to threats, a form of interaction that doesn't require direct conflict.
- terra-mystica (src-003): the proximity system turns adjacency into a genuine strategic tradeoff rather than either isolation or forced conflict.
- twilight-imperium-fourth-edition (src-003): strategy-card secondary effects usable by other players produced real alliance-and-betrayal dynamics in the reviewer's first game.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-007): Attack/interaction cards feel like a bolted-on answer to "the game needs more interaction," not a system-native one.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-005): Fully open information invites one player to dictate the group's choices.
- mage-knight (src-011): Competitive-mode interaction is experienced as denial and added variance from other players' actions rather than meaningful engagement; the co-op mode is described as "multiplayer solitaire."
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): Private simultaneous resolution trades away auditability — the same design that avoids downtime also removes the ability to verify others aren't cheating.
- dominion (src-003) and terra-mystica (src-002, src-004): two different euro shapes report the same bare complaint — low interaction, named directly rather than traced to a specific mechanism — with Terra Mystica additionally having no catch-up lever against a blocking-only counterplay option.
- dead-of-winter-the-long-night (src-004): the Crossroads-deck mechanism meant to generate social moments rarely triggered in actual play, undercutting its interaction design on paper.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): two-player "cooperative" play reads to the reviewer as closer to parallel solitaire than genuine collaboration.
- star-wars-rebellion (src-006): team mode technically supports four players but functionally collapses to two decision-makers, diluting the asymmetric 1v1 design it's built on top of.
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): higher player counts create spatial clutter that undercuts the 1v1 design's legibility.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-004): take-that bluffing/betrayal is explicitly a taste filter — the same design praised for tension will not suit every group.

## Coverage gaps

This pattern is no longer confined to cooperative or largely-parallel euro designs: hidden-role games (Nemesis, Shadows over Camelot), direct-conflict area-control games (Scythe, Star Wars: Rebellion), and trick-taking co-ops (The Crew) now all contribute. The clearest remaining gap is a game whose central mechanic is *negotiation or trading* rather than conflict, suspicion, or cooperation — Twilight Imperium's alliance dynamics are the closest case so far but are incidental to its strategy-card system rather than a dedicated trading/negotiation core.
