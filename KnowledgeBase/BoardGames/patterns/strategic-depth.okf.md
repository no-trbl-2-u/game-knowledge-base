## Summary

Strategic depth splits into two failure modes across the corpus: too much randomness swamping planning (Ark Nova's oversized deck, Heat's catch-up), and too little decision space once a system is mastered or under-modded (Spirit Island's "solved" late rounds, Heroes of Terrinoth's scarce clever plays, GI JOE's shallow synergy ceiling). The strongest praised solutions convert apparent randomness into accountable planning (Aeon's End's no-shuffle deck) or make mastery itself the depth — asymmetric opposing systems that must be learned, not just your own (Root). Evidence is broad (8 of 10 games contributed something usable) but individually thin per game — mostly single review quotes.

## Evidence by game

- aeons-end (src-004): No-shuffle deckbuilding turns luck into planned sequencing ("you set those combos in motion through meticulous planning"); a loss reads as "a bad choice," not a bad draw, confidence: high
- ark-nova (src-008): The 212-card zoo deck produces output randomness large enough that a reviewer "can't really plan ahead" and wanted "less cards, or more ways to churn through the deck", confidence: high
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): A customer reviewer noted "the card synergy is there but not deep enough that it takes too long to resolve" — synergy exists but hits a shallow ceiling, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): Roll to Review lists as a con that "early games without modules may feel overly simplistic for seasoned players," making base-game strategic depth conditional on add-on modules, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): iSlaytheDragon reports that "opportunities for clever play are scarce" and the four-verb action structure "can feel more administrative than playable", confidence: high
- root (src-006): Gideon's Gaming frames Root's asymmetry as both its "high skill ceiling" and its cost — "to become proficient at the game, you not only have to understand your own faction, but every other faction at the table", confidence: high
- spirit-island (src-005): Gideon's Gaming reports the base game "can potentially solve the game many rounds in advance" so "last rounds feel stale," even while calling it a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value", confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): Reviews frame Gearloc build depth and character-specific dice as the game's central appeal once past the learning curve, though this doc's own status is needs_followup, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): eliminating deck shuffling converts randomness into a planning problem, so losses are diagnosable as choice errors rather than luck.
- root (src-006): asymmetric faction engines create a "high skill ceiling" that reviewers explicitly praise as depth, not just complexity.
- spirit-island (src-005): despite late-game predictability complaints, the same review calls the system a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value."

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008): a large, high-variance card deck can outrun a player's ability to plan around it.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): card synergy exists but doesn't run deep enough for players seeking more from deck construction.
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): the unmodded base race can feel "overly simplistic" for experienced players — depth is bolted on via optional modules rather than built into the core loop.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): a narrow verb set and repetitive Rest/administration turns limit space for clever play.
- root (src-006): the same asymmetry that produces depth also produces a mastery burden — understanding every opponent faction, not just your own — that reviewers flag as a barrier.
- spirit-island (src-005): predictable base-game fear/blight cycling lets attentive players "solve" outcomes several rounds ahead, flattening late-game decisions.

## Coverage gaps

Two of the ten tagged games (arkham-horror-the-card-game, hoplomachus-remastered) carry the strategic-depth label but their reception docs had no claim actually about decision space, solved play, or choice quality — only onboarding/setup/rules-clarity evidence — so they were excluded here. The corpus would benefit from a heavier, non-cooperative strategy game with an explicit "dominant strategy" or "shallow choice" review complaint; current evidence skews toward co-op/deckbuilders and one area-control game, leaving competitive-euro-style strategic-depth failure underrepresented.
