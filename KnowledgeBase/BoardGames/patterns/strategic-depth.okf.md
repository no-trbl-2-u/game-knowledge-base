## Summary

Strategic depth splits into two failure modes across the corpus: too much randomness swamping planning (Ark Nova's oversized deck, Heat's catch-up), and too little decision space once a system is mastered, under-modded, or simply avoidable (Spirit Island's "solved" late rounds, Heroes of Terrinoth's scarce clever plays, GI JOE's shallow synergy ceiling, Elder Sign's zero-penalty avoidance of hard adventures). The strongest praised solutions convert apparent randomness into accountable planning (Aeon's End's no-shuffle deck) or make mastery itself the depth — asymmetric opposing systems that must be learned, not just your own (Root). A newer thread from campaign/solo-leaning games (Onirim, Bloodborne, Dead of Winter) adds a third failure mode: content pools too small to keep repeated play from going stale, distinct from randomness or a solved decision tree. Evidence is broad (13 of 15 candidate games contributed something usable) but individually thin per game — mostly single review quotes, and several new rows draw on needs_followup-status docs (Bloodborne, Dead of Winter: A Crossroads Game, Shadows over Camelot).

## Evidence by game

- aeons-end (src-004): No-shuffle deckbuilding turns luck into planned sequencing ("you set those combos in motion through meticulous planning"); a loss reads as "a bad choice," not a bad draw, confidence: high
- ark-nova (src-008): The 212-card zoo deck produces output randomness large enough that a reviewer "can't really plan ahead" and wanted "less cards, or more ways to churn through the deck", confidence: high
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): A customer reviewer noted "the card synergy is there but not deep enough that it takes too long to resolve" — synergy exists but hits a shallow ceiling, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): Roll to Review lists as a con that "early games without modules may feel overly simplistic for seasoned players," making base-game strategic depth conditional on add-on modules, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): iSlaytheDragon reports that "opportunities for clever play are scarce" and the four-verb action structure "can feel more administrative than playable", confidence: high
- root (src-006): Gideon's Gaming frames Root's asymmetry as both its "high skill ceiling" and its cost — "to become proficient at the game, you not only have to understand your own faction, but every other faction at the table", confidence: high
- spirit-island (src-005): Gideon's Gaming reports the base game "can potentially solve the game many rounds in advance" so "last rounds feel stale," even while calling it a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value", confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): Reviews frame Gearloc build depth and character-specific dice as the game's central appeal once past the learning curve, though this doc's own status is needs_followup, confidence: medium
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): reviewers praise the bluffing and risk-management core but report the standalone box's encounter, upgrade, and Final Boss pool becomes familiar quickly on repeat plays, confidence: high (source doc status: needs_followup)
- dark-pact (src-007, src-008, src-010): multiple reviews describe combinatorial chains as the game's central appeal, but critical reviews also report some chains create more handling than strategic payoff, confidence: medium
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): reviewer praises the 80-card Crossroads deck but flags that the story supply's visible finiteness undercuts repeated-play discovery and planning uncertainty for veteran players, confidence: medium (source doc status: needs_followup)
- earthborne-rangers (src-004, src-005): Space-Biff praises contextual card ecosystems for producing legible decision space, while Gideon's Gaming reports that the game's several concurrent subsystems (tabletop-RPG, prewritten-story, dynamic-storytelling) can step on each other's toes, confidence: medium
- elder-sign (src-002): a dominant no-risk strategy exists — hard adventures can simply be ignored with no penalty for avoidance, letting players "stash the tough monsters" undisturbed, confidence: high
- elder-sign (src-004): thematic engagement rather than mechanical depth carries the experience, so analytically-minded groups "will lose a bit of its luster," confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the base solo game is "not very challenging after several plays," with difficulty scaling relegated to expansion modules rather than available in the base box, confidence: medium
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): strategic depth rests on choosing the least-damaging evil progression and allocating limited hands across pressures, but one reviewer says these functional quest mechanics are "eclipsed by" the social Traitor system, confidence: medium (source doc status: needs_followup)

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): eliminating deck shuffling converts randomness into a planning problem, so losses are diagnosable as choice errors rather than luck.
- root (src-006): asymmetric faction engines create a "high skill ceiling" that reviewers explicitly praise as depth, not just complexity.
- spirit-island (src-005): despite late-game predictability complaints, the same review calls the system a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value."
- dark-pact (src-007, src-008, src-010): the combinatorial combo core is repeatedly named as the design's central strength, even by reviews that are otherwise critical.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008): a large, high-variance card deck can outrun a player's ability to plan around it.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): card synergy exists but doesn't run deep enough for players seeking more from deck construction.
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): the unmodded base race can feel "overly simplistic" for experienced players — depth is bolted on via optional modules rather than built into the core loop.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): a narrow verb set and repetitive Rest/administration turns limit space for clever play.
- root (src-006): the same asymmetry that produces depth also produces a mastery burden — understanding every opponent faction, not just your own — that reviewers flag as a barrier.
- spirit-island (src-005): predictable base-game fear/blight cycling lets attentive players "solve" outcomes several rounds ahead, flattening late-game decisions.
- elder-sign (src-002): the total absence of a penalty for skipping hard content lets players opt out of the game's stated challenge entirely.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the base game plateaus in difficulty quickly, with no in-box lever to restore challenge for experienced solo players.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): a strong core loop runs out of fresh content faster than reviewers wanted, given the standalone box's fixed pool.

## Coverage gaps

Two of the ten original tagged games (arkham-horror-the-card-game, hoplomachus-remastered) carry the strategic-depth label but their reception docs had no claim actually about decision space, solved play, or choice quality — only onboarding/setup/rules-clarity evidence — so they were excluded here. The-Crew and Scythe also carry the label in their frontmatter but their better-if docs' body claims never reach a distinct strategic-depth statement (onboarding, communication, and combat-pacing claims instead), so they were left out rather than stretched to fit. Bloodborne, Dead of Winter: A Crossroads Game, and Shadows over Camelot contribute only needs_followup-status evidence and should be reweighted once those reception docs clear review. The corpus still lacks a heavier, non-cooperative competitive strategy game with an explicit "dominant strategy" or "shallow choice" review complaint distinct from the several now-covered "content pool runs dry" cases.
