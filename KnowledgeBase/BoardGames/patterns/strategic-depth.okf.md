## Summary

Strategic depth splits into two failure modes across the corpus: too much randomness swamping planning (Ark Nova's oversized deck, Heat's catch-up), and too little decision space once a system is mastered, under-modded, or simply avoidable (Spirit Island's "solved" late rounds, Heroes of Terrinoth's scarce clever plays, GI JOE's shallow synergy ceiling, and — newly evidenced this pass — Elder Sign's dominant no-risk strategy of ignoring hard adventures outright, and Forgotten Waters' routine checks that stay "sameish" outside a few tactically demanding moments). The strongest praised solutions convert apparent randomness into accountable planning (Aeon's End's no-shuffle deck) or make mastery itself the depth — asymmetric opposing systems that must be learned, not just your own (Root). A new thread this pass is thematic engagement substituting for mechanical depth: two reviewers (Elder Sign, Shadows over Camelot) independently describe their games as carried by narrative/social tension rather than by their functional decision system, which the reviews treat as a legitimate design choice rather than a flaw, provided the game is not sold on mechanical depth it doesn't have. Evidence is broad (14 of 16 tagged games contributed something usable) but individually thin per game — mostly single review quotes.

## Evidence by game

- aeons-end (src-004): No-shuffle deckbuilding turns luck into planned sequencing ("you set those combos in motion through meticulous planning"); a loss reads as "a bad choice," not a bad draw, confidence: high
- ark-nova (src-008): The 212-card zoo deck produces output randomness large enough that a reviewer "can't really plan ahead" and wanted "less cards, or more ways to churn through the deck", confidence: high
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): reviewers praise the bluffing/risk-management core but report that the standalone box's limited encounter, upgrade, and Final Boss pool becomes familiar quickly, confidence: high
- dark-pact (src-007, src-008, src-010): multiple reviews describe administrative pressure from sequencing and Action-counting during long turns, and separately describe some combo chains as producing more handling than strategic payoff, confidence: medium
- elder-sign (src-002): a dominant, cost-free avoidance strategy exists — hard adventures can simply be left unattempted and stashed with no penalty, confidence: high
- elder-sign (src-004): the game's engagement is carried more by thematic immersion than mechanical depth, so "dry, analytical" groups get less from it than groups willing to imagine the fiction, confidence: high
- forgotten-waters (src-012): the reviewer calls much of play "sameish," with only critical moments feeling tactically demanding against otherwise repetitive skill-check/resource choices, confidence: high
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): A customer reviewer noted "the card synergy is there but not deep enough that it takes too long to resolve" — synergy exists but hits a shallow ceiling, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): Roll to Review lists as a con that "early games without modules may feel overly simplistic for seasoned players," making base-game strategic depth conditional on add-on modules, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): iSlaytheDragon reports that "opportunities for clever play are scarce" and the four-verb action structure "can feel more administrative than playable", confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the base solo game is reported as not very challenging after several plays, with difficulty scaling currently reliant on expansion modules rather than the base rules, confidence: medium
- root (src-006): Gideon's Gaming frames Root's asymmetry as both its "high skill ceiling" and its cost — "to become proficient at the game, you not only have to understand your own faction, but every other faction at the table", confidence: high
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): reviewers describe the quest system's dual evil/heroic choices as functional and interesting, but say they are eclipsed by the social Traitor system as the base game's real strategic and emotional core, confidence: medium
- spirit-island (src-005): Gideon's Gaming reports the base game "can potentially solve the game many rounds in advance" so "last rounds feel stale," even while calling it a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value", confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): Reviews frame Gearloc build depth and character-specific dice as the game's central appeal once past the learning curve, though this doc's own status is needs_followup, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): eliminating deck shuffling converts randomness into a planning problem, so losses are diagnosable as choice errors rather than luck.
- root (src-006): asymmetric faction engines create a "high skill ceiling" that reviewers explicitly praise as depth, not just complexity.
- spirit-island (src-005): despite late-game predictability complaints, the same review calls the system a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value."
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): functional quest-choice mechanics coexist with, rather than compete against, a genuinely strong social-deduction core — reviewers treat the Traitor system as carrying the game, not as covering for a weak quest layer.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008): a large, high-variance card deck can outrun a player's ability to plan around it.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): a strong core loop runs out of content variety faster than the design's replay ambitions imply, for the standalone box specifically.
- dark-pact (src-007, src-008, src-010): some combinatorial chains cost more table time to resolve than they return in meaningful decisions.
- elder-sign (src-002): a zero-penalty avoidance option undercuts the risk/reward premise for any adventure a player judges too hard.
- forgotten-waters (src-012): routine resolution repeats often enough that most decisions read as interchangeable, with tactical demand concentrated in a minority of moments.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): card synergy exists but doesn't run deep enough for players seeking more from deck construction.
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): the unmodded base race can feel "overly simplistic" for experienced players — depth is bolted on via optional modules rather than built into the core loop.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): a narrow verb set and repetitive Rest/administration turns limit space for clever play.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): repeated solo plays outrun the base game's own difficulty ceiling.
- root (src-006): the same asymmetry that produces depth also produces a mastery burden — understanding every opponent faction, not just your own — that reviewers flag as a barrier.
- spirit-island (src-005): predictable base-game fear/blight cycling lets attentive players "solve" outcomes several rounds ahead, flattening late-game decisions.

## Coverage gaps

Two of the pre-2026-08 tagged games (arkham-horror-the-card-game, hoplomachus-remastered) carry the strategic-depth label but their reception docs had no claim actually about decision space, solved play, or choice quality — only onboarding/setup/rules-clarity evidence — so they were excluded here. Dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game and nemesis-lockdown carry the label this pass with claims that map more precisely to randomness (finite narrative supply; randomness overriding strategy) and are cited there instead. The corpus would benefit from a heavier, non-cooperative strategy game with an explicit "dominant strategy" or "shallow choice" review complaint; current evidence still skews toward co-op/deckbuilders and one area-control game, leaving competitive-euro-style strategic-depth failure underrepresented.
