## Summary

Strategic depth splits into three failure modes across the corpus (now 25+
games): too much randomness swamping planning (Ark Nova's oversized deck,
Heat's catch-up, Elder Sign's dice-gated tasks), too little decision space
once a system is mastered, under-modded, or solo-repeated (Spirit Island's
"solved" late rounds, Heroes of Terrinoth's scarce clever plays, GI JOE's
shallow synergy ceiling, Onirim's fading solo challenge, Forgotten Waters'
"sameish" routine choices), and a subtler failure specific to heavier
systems-games: real depth that exists but is *hidden* — a dominant core
loop at the center (Gaia Project, SETI, Terra Mystica) that reviewers can't
see until a reviewer or second playthrough surfaces it, or depth concentrated
in one subsystem that eclipses the rest (Shadows over Camelot's Traitor
suspicion outshining its quest mechanics). The strongest praised solutions
convert apparent randomness into accountable planning (Aeon's End's
no-shuffle deck), make mastery itself the depth (Root's asymmetric opposing
factions, Unmatched's per-fighter identity), or build genuinely deterministic,
low-luck planning systems that reviewers credit directly (Terra Mystica,
Terraforming Mars, Star Wars: Rebellion's mission-card/hidden-base chase).
Evidence is now broad across co-op, campaign, area-control, and heavy-euro
shapes, though still individually thin per game — mostly single review quotes.

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): eliminating deck shuffling converts randomness into a planning problem, so losses are diagnosable as choice errors rather than luck.
- root (src-006): asymmetric faction engines create a "high skill ceiling" that reviewers explicitly praise as depth, not just complexity.
- spirit-island (src-005): despite late-game predictability complaints, the same review calls the system a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value."
- terra-mystica (src-003, src-004): deterministic, low-luck planning across 14 factions and fully interconnected economic systems is credited directly as the game's depth, not as a byproduct of complexity.
- terraforming-mars (src-003): project-card variety is named as the engine-building identity itself, rewarding planning rather than obscuring it.
- star-wars-rebellion (src-005, src-006): the mission-card system and hidden-base search/chase are the parts of the game reviewers repeatedly call out as genuinely exciting strategic systems.
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): per-fighter asymmetry gives the system a fast-teaching surface with a real skill ceiling underneath — mastery is in learning each fighter's identity, echoing Root's "learn every faction" depth without the long onboarding tax.
- scythe (src-003): the progression ramp is explicitly praised for building in strategic complexity over the course of a session rather than front-loading or flattening it.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008): a large, high-variance card deck can outrun a player's ability to plan around it.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): card synergy exists but doesn't run deep enough for players seeking more from deck construction.
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): the unmodded base race can feel "overly simplistic" for experienced players — depth is bolted on via optional modules rather than built into the core loop.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): a narrow verb set and repetitive Rest/administration turns limit space for clever play.
- root (src-006): the same asymmetry that produces depth also produces a mastery burden — understanding every opponent faction, not just your own — that reviewers flag as a barrier.
- spirit-island (src-005): predictable base-game fear/blight cycling lets attentive players "solve" outcomes several rounds ahead, flattening late-game decisions.
- elder-sign (src-002): a risk-free avoidance strategy removes the central tension the dice/card system is built around.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): solo difficulty fades with repetition, and the base game has no built-in answer beyond buying expansion modules.
- forgotten-waters (src-012): most individual decisions are "sameish," with depth concentrated only in rare critical story beats.
- dark-pact (src-008, src-010): some of the game's own combo chains cost more table time than the strategic payoff they deliver.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): the quest layer that should carry strategic depth is "eclipsed by" the social deduction layer built on top of it.
- nemesis-lockdown (src-009): rules intricacy can obscure the meaningful decisions underneath its praised narrative variety.
- gloomhaven-jaws-of-the-lion (src-002, src-003): ability-hoarding against card loss/exhaustion is real depth, but reviewers flag it as mental overhead rather than pure enjoyment.

## Coverage gaps

Two of the originally-tagged games (arkham-horror-the-card-game, hoplomachus-remastered) still carry the strategic-depth label but their reception docs have no claim actually about decision space, solved play, or choice quality — only onboarding/setup/rules-clarity evidence — so they remain excluded here. The earlier gap (competitive-euro-style strategic-depth underrepresented) is now substantially filled by Terra Mystica, Terraforming Mars, Scythe, Twilight Imperium, and Star Wars: Rebellion; the newer gap is the "depth hidden behind a bad first impression" shape (Gaia Project, SETI) — a good WISHLIST candidate would be a heavy euro whose reviewers explicitly describe *discovering* depth only on a second playthrough, to test whether that's a teaching problem or a design one.
