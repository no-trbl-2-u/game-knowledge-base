## Summary

Strategic depth splits into two dominant failure modes across the corpus, plus a
third, related one. The most common is too little decision space once a system is
mastered, under-modded, or simply runs out of content: finite card pools and boss
rosters expose their limits quickly (Bloodborne's 32-card upgrade deck and five
bosses, Dead of Winter: A Crossroads Game's finite Crossroads deck, GI JOE's shallow
synergy ceiling), and even richer systems can flatten into "sameish" play outside a
few critical moments (Forgotten Waters, Spirit Island's solved late rounds, Heroes of
Terrinoth's scarce clever plays). The second is too much randomness swamping planning
(Ark Nova's oversized deck, Heat's catch-up). The third is complexity that
masquerades as depth without delivering it: Nemesis: Lockdown's rules intricacy is
named as actively obscuring meaningful decisions, and Dark Pact's long combos deliver
"comparatively modest payoff" for the setup they require. The strongest praised
solutions convert apparent randomness into accountable planning (Aeon's End's
no-shuffle deck), make mastery itself the depth — asymmetric opposing systems that
must be learned, not just your own (Root) — or build a legible risk/reward tension
into a single core action (Unmatched's attacks mixing "brute force and light
bluffing" around when to invest cards). Evidence is broad but individually thin per
game — mostly single review quotes.

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): eliminating deck shuffling converts randomness into a planning problem, so losses are diagnosable as choice errors rather than luck.
- root (src-006): asymmetric faction engines create a "high skill ceiling" that reviewers explicitly praise as depth, not just complexity.
- spirit-island (src-005): despite late-game predictability complaints, the same review calls the system a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value."
- unmatched-battle-of-legends-volume-one (src-003): combat framed as "a mix of brute force and light bluffing" turns the timing of when to invest cards into a recurring source of depth rather than a fixed formula.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008): a large, high-variance card deck can outrun a player's ability to plan around it.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): card synergy exists but doesn't run deep enough for players seeking more from deck construction.
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): the unmodded base race can feel "overly simplistic" for experienced players — depth is bolted on via optional modules rather than built into the core loop.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): a narrow verb set and repetitive Rest/administration turns limit space for clever play.
- root (src-006): the same asymmetry that produces depth also produces a mastery burden — understanding every opponent faction, not just your own — that reviewers flag as a barrier.
- spirit-island (src-005): predictable base-game fear/blight cycling lets attentive players "solve" outcomes several rounds ahead, flattening late-game decisions.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): the 32-card upgrade deck and five bosses "expose most content quickly," a low content ceiling once the pool is known.
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): the finite Crossroads deck "makes it want more cards," running out of narrative decision-fuel ahead of replay demand.
- forgotten-waters (src-012): outside a handful of tactically demanding critical moments, "much play [is] sameish," so most of a session doesn't test decisions the way its climactic beats do.
- nemesis-lockdown (src-009): rules intricacy "can obscure meaningful decisions" — added complexity without added choice.

## Coverage gaps

arkham-horror-the-card-game and hoplomachus-remastered carry the strategic-depth label but their reception docs had no claim actually about decision space, solved play, or choice quality — only onboarding/setup/rules-clarity evidence — so they remain excluded here. The corpus would still benefit from a heavier, non-cooperative strategy game with an explicit "dominant strategy" or "shallow choice" review complaint; current evidence still skews toward co-op/narrative/deckbuilding designs (Bloodborne, Dark Pact, Dead of Winter, Forgotten Waters, Nemesis: Lockdown) and one head-to-head skirmish game (Unmatched) rather than a competitive Euro, leaving that segment's strategic-depth failure mode underrepresented.
