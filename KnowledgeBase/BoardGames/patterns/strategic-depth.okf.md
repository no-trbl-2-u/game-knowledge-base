## Summary

Strategic depth splits into two failure modes across the corpus: too much randomness swamping planning (Ark Nova's oversized deck, Heat's catch-up, Elder Sign's fully dice-gated resolution), and too little decision space once a system is mastered, under-modded, or has a dominant no-risk line (Spirit Island's "solved" late rounds, Heroes of Terrinoth's scarce clever plays, GI JOE's shallow synergy ceiling, Elder Sign's penalty-free avoidance of hard adventures, Forgotten Waters' repetitive routine checks). The strongest praised solutions convert apparent randomness into accountable planning (Aeon's End's no-shuffle deck) or make mastery itself the depth — asymmetric opposing systems that must be learned, not just your own (Root). A new thread across the added evidence: thematic/social systems can substitute for mechanical depth entirely, for better or worse — Elder Sign's mood carries analytically-minded groups less well than its dice math would suggest, and Shadows over Camelot's quest arithmetic is functional but explicitly eclipsed by its social Traitor system. Evidence is broad (14 of 16 tagged games contributed something usable) but individually thin per game — mostly single review quotes.

## Evidence by game

- aeons-end (src-004): No-shuffle deckbuilding turns luck into planned sequencing ("you set those combos in motion through meticulous planning"); a loss reads as "a bad choice," not a bad draw, confidence: high
- ark-nova (src-008): The 212-card zoo deck produces output randomness large enough that a reviewer "can't really plan ahead" and wanted "less cards, or more ways to churn through the deck", confidence: high
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): the standalone box's core encounter/upgrade/Final Boss pool is limited, so reviewers report the strong bluffing-and-risk core "becomes familiar quickly" on repeat play, confidence: high
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): a reviewer who praises the 80-card Crossroads deck still names its visibly finite supply as a repeat-play predictability concern, confidence: medium
- elder-sign (src-002): a dominant no-risk strategy exists — hard adventures can simply be avoided "with no penalty," confidence: high
- elder-sign (src-004): thematic engagement carries the experience more than mechanical depth, so "if your players tend toward the dry, analytical types, the game can be entertaining but it will lose a bit of its luster," confidence: high
- forgotten-waters (src-012): routine skill-check and resource choices are called "sameish," with tactical weight concentrated only in critical moments, confidence: high
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): A customer reviewer noted "the card synergy is there but not deep enough that it takes too long to resolve" — synergy exists but hits a shallow ceiling, confidence: medium
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): Roll to Review lists as a con that "early games without modules may feel overly simplistic for seasoned players," making base-game strategic depth conditional on add-on modules, confidence: medium
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): iSlaytheDragon reports that "opportunities for clever play are scarce" and the four-verb action structure "can feel more administrative than playable", confidence: high
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the base solo game is "not very challenging after several plays," with difficulty scaling currently reserved for expansion modules rather than available in the base box, confidence: medium
- root (src-006): Gideon's Gaming frames Root's asymmetry as both its "high skill ceiling" and its cost — "to become proficient at the game, you not only have to understand your own faction, but every other faction at the table", confidence: high
- shadows-over-camelot (src-006, src-007): depth rests in choosing the least damaging evil progression and allocating limited hands across pressures, but the social Traitor system eclipses this quest arithmetic, so revisions shouldn't mistake more quest mechanics for the witnessed core appeal, confidence: medium
- spirit-island (src-005): Gideon's Gaming reports the base game "can potentially solve the game many rounds in advance" so "last rounds feel stale," even while calling it a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value", confidence: high
- too-many-bones (src-004, src-005): Reviews frame Gearloc build depth and character-specific dice as the game's central appeal once past the learning curve, though this doc's own status is needs_followup, confidence: medium

## Where it works

- aeons-end (src-004): eliminating deck shuffling converts randomness into a planning problem, so losses are diagnosable as choice errors rather than luck.
- root (src-006): asymmetric faction engines create a "high skill ceiling" that reviewers explicitly praise as depth, not just complexity.
- spirit-island (src-005): despite late-game predictability complaints, the same review calls the system a "high strategy game with a ton of variety, player choice, and replay value."
- shadows-over-camelot (src-007): even eclipsed by the Traitor system, the underlying quest mechanics are independently called "functional and interesting" rather than filler.

## Where it fails

- ark-nova (src-008): a large, high-variance card deck can outrun a player's ability to plan around it.
- bloodborne-the-card-game (src-005, src-006): a limited standalone content pool means the praised bluffing/risk core becomes familiar quickly on repeat plays.
- dead-of-winter-a-crossroads-game (src-005): repeat players can perceive the Crossroads card supply as visibly finite, weakening discovery/planning uncertainty over time.
- elder-sign (src-002): hard adventures can be freely avoided with no mechanical penalty, undercutting the game's own difficulty curve.
- elder-sign (src-004): the experience leans on theme rather than mechanical depth, so analytically-minded groups get comparatively less out of it.
- forgotten-waters (src-012): most routine choices are interchangeable ("sameish"), with real tactical demand concentrated in only a few critical moments per session.
- gi-joe-deck-building-game (src-005): card synergy exists but doesn't run deep enough for players seeking more from deck construction.
- heat-pedal-to-the-metal (src-005): the unmodded base race can feel "overly simplistic" for experienced players — depth is bolted on via optional modules rather than built into the core loop.
- heroes-of-terrinoth (src-006): a narrow verb set and repetitive Rest/administration turns limit space for clever play.
- onirim-second-edition (src-002): the base game plateaus in challenge after a handful of plays, with the only escape hatch being purchased expansion modules rather than in-box difficulty scaling.
- root (src-006): the same asymmetry that produces depth also produces a mastery burden — understanding every opponent faction, not just your own — that reviewers flag as a barrier.
- shadows-over-camelot (src-007): the game's own quest-choice arithmetic is functional but eclipsed by its social system, meaning the mechanical depth alone would not carry the design.
- spirit-island (src-005): predictable base-game fear/blight cycling lets attentive players "solve" outcomes several rounds ahead, flattening late-game decisions.

## Coverage gaps

Two of the fifteen tagged games (arkham-horror-the-card-game, hoplomachus-remastered) carry the strategic-depth label but their reception docs had no claim actually about decision space, solved play, or choice quality — only onboarding/setup/rules-clarity evidence — so they were excluded here. Nemesis-Lockdown's randomness claim touches strategic depth ("complexity can obscure meaningful choices") but the doc doesn't stand this up as its own labeled claim distinct from its randomness citation, so it was left out of this table rather than double-counted. The corpus would benefit from a heavier, non-cooperative strategy game with an explicit "dominant strategy" or "shallow choice" review complaint; current evidence skews toward co-op/deckbuilders and campaign titles, leaving competitive-euro-style strategic-depth failure underrepresented even as the co-op/campaign side of this pattern has grown considerably deeper.
