## Summary

Thin evidence (1 game). The single data point (The Quacks of Quedlinburg) suggests
a catch-up mechanism can keep trailing players *feeling* competitive through the
midgame even while it fails to fully prevent a leader from becoming apparent late.
One reviewer praised the catch-up system for keeping players in contention, while
another reviewer of the same game observed that by round 7/8 the eventual winner
is usually already clear and other players may be unable to affect the outcome.
This looks less like a contradiction than a timing boundary: the mechanism works
early/mid-game but its effect fades in the final rounds. Too small a sample to
generalize beyond this one title.

## Evidence by game

## Where it works

the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-006): reviewer credits the catch-up mechanism
with keeping trailing players competitive rather than checked out, per the
reviews.okf.md "Praised design"/"Source-backed facts" claim ("The catch-up
mechanic does a great job of keeping players competitive").

## Where it fails

the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): the better-if.okf.md doc flags that
comeback pressure does not hold up deep into the final rounds — by round
7/8 the likely winner is often already apparent, undercutting the earlier
catch-up effect.

## Coverage gaps

This pattern needs more games before it can be treated as a real cross-game
synthesis rather than a single case study. A good WISHLIST candidate would be
a competitive area-control or engine-building game with an *explicit*
runaway-leader complaint in its reviews (e.g. titles where reviewers describe
snowballing lead advantages or a lack of catch-up/rubber-banding mechanisms),
to contrast against Quacks' partial mitigation via its catch-up system.
