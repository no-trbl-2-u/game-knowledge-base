## Summary

Evidence remains narrow (2 games) but now spans two different genres with a
shared shape: a runaway-leader problem is tolerated by reviewers when the
leading advantage is *telegraphed* — visible to opponents in time to react —
even if no formal catch-up mechanism exists. The Quacks of Quedlinburg's
explicit catch-up mechanism keeps trailing players feeling competitive
through the midgame but fades by round 7/8. Food Chain Magnate has no
catch-up mechanism at all, yet its reviewer frames small early
production/sales mistakes compounding into a "hopelessly behind" position as
tolerable specifically because the trouble is telegraphed and opponents'
moves are visible — the complaint is about onboarding new players into
reading those signals, not about the runaway-leader dynamic itself being
unfair. Still too small a sample to generalize confidently beyond these two
titles.

## Evidence by game

- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-006): What's Eric Playing praises the catch-up
  mechanic for "keeping players competitive" during play, confidence: high
- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): the same reviewer notes that "from round
  7/8... it's usually pretty clear who is going to win," with other players
  potentially unable to affect it — a better-if opportunity to "preserve
  comeback pressure deeper into the final rounds", confidence: medium
- food-chain-magnate (src-003, src-004): official rules tie up marketeers for campaign
  duration with ongoing salary costs; the reviewer says small production/sales
  differences can cause "long-term setbacks" and trouble is telegraphed, but mistakes
  can leave a player "hopelessly behind," confidence: medium

## Where it works

- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-006): reviewer credits the catch-up mechanism
  with keeping trailing players competitive rather than checked out, per the
  reviews.okf.md "Praised design"/"Source-backed facts" claim ("The catch-up
  mechanic does a great job of keeping players competitive").
- food-chain-magnate (src-004): the reviewer praises telegraphed threats and visible
  opponent moves ("clues about the market") as the counterplay that makes a leaderless
  catch-up mechanism tolerable — players can see trouble coming even without a
  rubber-band system to fix it.

## Where it fails

- the-quacks-of-quedlinburg (src-005): the better-if.okf.md doc flags that
  comeback pressure does not hold up deep into the final rounds — by round
  7/8 the likely winner is often already apparent, undercutting the earlier
  catch-up effect.
- food-chain-magnate (src-003, src-004): with no catch-up mechanism at all, a new
  player's early misreads compound into a position the reviewer calls "hopelessly
  behind" well before the game ends — the better-if gap here is onboarding players
  into reading the telegraphed signals, not adding a comeback mechanic that would
  undercut the design's intentionally harsh economy.

## Coverage gaps

Two games now show the same telegraphing-over-rubber-banding shape from opposite
design choices (an explicit catch-up tool that fades, and no catch-up tool at all
compensated by visibility), but both are still individually thin evidence. A good
WISHLIST candidate remains a competitive area-control or engine-building game with an
*explicit* runaway-leader complaint where the leading advantage is reported as hidden
or un-telegraphed, to test whether visibility is really the load-bearing variable or
just correlated with these two titles.
