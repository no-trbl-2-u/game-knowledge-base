## Better-if opportunities

- Claim: Star Wars: Rebellion could be easier to onboard if its rule exceptions, card-driven procedures, and first-round priorities were staged for new players.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: Ars Technica reports that detailed rules and scattered exceptions require repeated lookup; Geeks Under Grace says the game demands advanced rules knowledge and a large learning investment.
  Confidence: high
  Interpretation: A teaching layer could reduce search cost without removing the underlying faction asymmetry.

- Claim: The game could be easier to operate if its table footprint, setup, and component handling were better signposted.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: Ars Technica describes two boards, 153 miniatures, and an afternoon-scale session; Geeks Under Grace says a large table is required and comments on component handling.
  Confidence: high
  Interpretation: This is an operational and accessibility opportunity, not evidence that the board should be made smaller by default.

- Claim: Late combat could be better if it resolved more quickly or offered more consequential choices.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: Ars Technica calls combat fiddly and says multi-round, multi-theater battles become tedious; Geeks Under Grace calls the endgame combat a high-maintenance slugfest and says the cards rarely create interesting decisions.
  Confidence: high
  Interpretation: The reception supports examining combat resolution and late-game pacing, while preserving the threat of battle that reviewers praise.

- Claim: The Rebel endgame could feel more dramatic if its reputation victory had a stronger visible climax.
  Source: src-005
  Evidence: Ars Technica praises the Imperial destruction finish but says Rebel victory is represented by a marker meeting the round tracker and lacks the same drama.
  Confidence: medium
  Interpretation: This is a presentation and endgame-framing opportunity, not proof that the asymmetric win condition is unsound.

- Claim: Four-player team mode may be better when the product explicitly teaches its reduced agency and slower pace.
  Source: src-006
  Evidence: Geeks Under Grace lists the game as “2–4 (but really 2)” and says splitting leaders between teammates reduces agency; Ars Technica calls it a two-player game at heart.
  Confidence: high
  Interpretation: The evidence supports clearer player-count expectation-setting rather than treating four-player play as an equivalent mode.
