## Summary

Players who enjoy the structure value the conversion of cooperation from a moral default into a strategic transaction. The common threat gives bargaining force to contribution, restraint to aggression, instability to alliances, and consequence to greed.

## Praised dynamics

### 1. Cooperation becomes a strategic choice

- Claim: Players help because continued survival preserves their own chance to win, making contributions calculable sacrifices rather than unconditional duties.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: *Archipelago*’s rebellion burden is described as a “tax” on continuing the game.
  Confidence: high

### 2. Self-interest and survival collide every turn

- Claim: The structure repeatedly forces players to choose among improving position, rescuing the group, compelling someone else to rescue it, and letting the crisis approach its limit.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: the review’s account of private goals under a shared rebellion threshold supplies all four competing pressures.
  Confidence: high

### 3. Betrayal gains meaningful timing

- Claim: Attacking or withholding too early can kill the aggressor along with the group; waiting too long can concede the sole prize.
  Source: src-002, src-003
  Evidence: both *Cutthroat Caverns* reviews center successful play on timing the turn from cooperation to betrayal.
  Confidence: high

### 4. Negotiation arises from a concrete shared burden

- Claim: The common threat gives the table substantive bargaining questions: who pays, who sacrifices an action, who receives compensation, and who is responsible for the next crisis.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: rebellion control is a shared cost distributed among players with unequal incentives to pay it.
  Confidence: high

### 5. Extortion and brinkmanship create political leverage

- Claim: A trailing or resource-poor player can demand compensation for preventing collapse, turning survival itself into a negotiable asset.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: the “tax” framing means willingness to contribute can be withheld or exchanged.
  Confidence: medium

### 6. Alliances remain unstable

- Claim: The common enemy creates temporary alliances while the sole-winner condition prevents those alliances from hardening into permanent teams.
  Source: src-003
  Evidence: party survival requires assistance even while players compete for the rewards that determine victory.
  Confidence: high

### 7. Suspicion can emerge without a formal traitor

- Claim: Ordinary self-interest makes resource conservation ambiguous: it may be prudent play, an attempt to shift cost, or preparation to overtake the table.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: hidden scoring makes apparently irrational actions difficult to interpret.
  Confidence: high

### 8. Hidden scoring becomes psychologically stronger

- Claim: When standings are uncertain, players cannot calculate exactly who should pay most for common survival and must infer positions from behavior.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: the review praises private goals and the ambiguity they create around motives.
  Confidence: high

### 9. The mechanic rewards player-reading

- Claim: Strategic assessment expands from material position to beliefs and temperament: who thinks they lead, who bluffs weakness, and who is willing to risk collapse.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: hidden objectives make behavior an imperfect signal of both standing and intent.
  Confidence: high

### 10. The shared threat can restrain the leader

- Claim: The player with the strongest chance of individual victory may rationally bear more of the survival burden because that player has the most to lose from collective defeat.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: the review explicitly links stronger positions to greater incentive to pay the rebellion tax.
  Confidence: high

### 11. Trailing players retain political leverage

- Claim: A player behind on score can remain indispensable to the next crisis and trade that indispensability for resources, favors, or restraint.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: common survival depends on contributions not perfectly correlated with personal score.
  Confidence: medium

### 12. Outcomes can remain uncertain without direct catch-up points

- Claim: Leaders spending on survival while weaker players conserve resources or demand compensation can create comeback paths without awarding arbitrary bonuses.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: unequal incentives to fund the shared tax naturally transfer tempo away from the leader.
  Confidence: medium

### 13. Shared success can soften personal defeat

- Claim: Some players experience losing the ranking as less severe because they still helped defeat the villain or preserve the world.
  Source: src-004
  Evidence: indexed *Legendary* discussion described the ending as a group victory with one best performer.
  Confidence: medium

### 14. The structure generates memorable stories

- Claim: The incentives naturally produce stories of leaders funding survival, holdouts bargaining over disaster, last-minute rescue, and betrayal immediately after the common enemy falls.
  Source: src-001, src-002, src-003
  Evidence: the reviewed systems repeatedly join common danger to hidden goals or reward theft.
  Confidence: medium

### 15. Theme can justify conflicted loyalty

- Claim: Rival corporations, governments, political factions, and desperate adventurers fit the structure because they plausibly need one another while retaining incompatible ambitions.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: both *CO₂* reviews connect private economic competition to a shared environmental survival condition.
  Confidence: high

### 16. Greed acquires systemic consequences

- Claim: Selfish optimization can damage the world or institution that makes victory possible, forcing players to distinguish profitable greed from suicidal greed.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: profit-seeking in *CO₂* is bounded by collective ecological collapse.
  Confidence: high

### 17. Take-that can feel less arbitrary

- Claim: Aggressive interference is easier to accept when backstabbing is the announced premise rather than an unexpected interruption to an otherwise peaceful game.
  Source: src-002
  Evidence: the review praises *Cutthroat Caverns* because betrayal is its central contract.
  Confidence: high

### 18. Cruelty can be dramatic without permanent villains

- Claim: Players can threaten, bargain, defect, and reconcile without assigning one person a fixed antagonist role for the whole session.
  Source: src-001, src-003
  Evidence: current incentives, not permanent teams, govern cooperation in both reviewed structures.
  Confidence: medium
