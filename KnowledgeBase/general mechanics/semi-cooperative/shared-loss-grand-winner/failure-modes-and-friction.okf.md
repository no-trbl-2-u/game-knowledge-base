## Summary

The central defect is terminal leverage: a player who no longer expects to win may rationally prefer common ruin, or may gain more influence from threatening ruin than from playing the rest of the system. Around that defect gather kingmaking, free-riding, coerced sacrifice, unstable expectations, and group-dependent conflict.

## Failure modes and disliked dynamics

### 1. A player who cannot win may make everyone lose

- Claim: Once personal victory appears impossible, collective survival may cease to offer a rational benefit and deliberate collapse may become the player’s strongest remaining influence.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: indexed post excerpt: games where everyone can lose but only one can win make it “terribly easy to throw the game to prevent anyone winning.”
  Confidence: medium

### 2. The structure can reward spite

- Claim: Formal self-interest and emotional retaliation become difficult to distinguish when “if I cannot win, neither can you” is achievable by refusing survival costs.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: the cited concern is specifically about throwing the common game to deny every rival a win.
  Confidence: medium

### 3. It can produce kingmaking

- Claim: A trailing player may be unable to win yet still decide who pays, who receives aid, whether the group survives, and which leader gets the final opening.
  Source: src-001, src-006
  Evidence: shared failure plus unequal contribution leverage gives a noncontender decisive control over the surviving contenders.
  Confidence: medium

### 4. Extortion can eclipse the rest of the strategy

- Claim: Threatening collective defeat may become so powerful that buying off the least cooperative player dominates the game’s intended systems.
  Source: src-001, src-006
  Evidence: survival can be withheld, while the rebellion burden is explicitly a cost of keeping the contest alive.
  Confidence: medium

### 5. Free-riding creates resentment

- Claim: Players can delay contribution in the expectation that someone later in turn order will be forced to pay or end the session.
  Source: src-006
  Evidence: the review’s shared rebellion burden creates incentives to shift the tax to other players.
  Confidence: medium

This is the recurring **Kill Doctor Lucky problem**: everyone waits for somebody else to perform the necessary act, leaving the final actor to sacrifice or accept common defeat.

### 6. The cooperative claim can feel fraudulent

- Claim: Shared punishment alone may not feel cooperative when players cannot positively assist one another and are merely penalized for collective inefficiency.
  Source: src-002
  Evidence: the discussion disputes classifying *CO₂* as cooperative merely because everyone can lose together.
  Confidence: medium

### 7. Sole-winner scoring can contaminate shared success

- Claim: Some groups enjoy defeating the common enemy and resent being told afterward that only the highest scorer truly won.
  Source: src-003, src-004
  Evidence: *Legendary* is repeatedly treated as a cooperative deckbuilder even though its rules support post-villain point comparison.
  Confidence: medium

### 8. Sacrifice can feel mechanically foolish

- Claim: A player who repeatedly saves the group may consume the resources needed for personal victory while a free-rider conserves resources and wins.
  Source: src-006
  Evidence: paying the common rebellion tax imposes an unequal personal opportunity cost.
  Confidence: medium

### 9. Collective defeat can make hours of planning feel wasted

- Claim: Long-range personal planning can feel invalidated rather than defeated when other players fail to control the global system.
  Source: src-005
  Evidence: the archived *CO₂* review criticizes extensive planning being erased by collective pollution failure.
  Confidence: high

### 10. One careless player can destroy everyone’s game

- Claim: A sensitive failure track makes onboarding dangerous because an inexperienced or inattentive action can cross the terminal threshold for the whole table.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: both accounts emphasize individual plans being exposed to collective track management.
  Confidence: medium

### 11. The safe boundary may be too difficult to read

- Claim: If consequences are opaque, delayed, or volatile, calculated brinkmanship becomes accidental suicide.
  Source: src-006
  Evidence: the review describes *Archipelago*’s rebellion balance as delicate and readily derailed.
  Confidence: high

### 12. Randomness can make failure feel arbitrary

- Claim: Random events that sharply advance the common threat prevent players from estimating how much selfishness the system can tolerate.
  Source: src-006
  Evidence: reception concerns around rebellion volatility undermine controlled pressure on the shared track.
  Confidence: medium

### 13. A weak common threat makes cooperation ceremonial

- Claim: When collective success is nearly automatic, players pursue points while the semi-cooperative layer becomes theater.
  Source: src-007
  Evidence: the review criticizes the base *Legendary* Mastermind as too easy for experienced groups.
  Confidence: high

### 14. An overpowering common threat makes competition ceremonial

- Claim: If nearly every resource must go toward survival, personal scoring becomes decoration on an awkward cooperative puzzle.
  Source: src-005, src-006
  Evidence: cross-source synthesis from the opportunity costs imposed by severe global failure systems.
  Confidence: medium

### 15. Negotiation can collapse into repetitive haggling

- Claim: Recurring crises may reproduce the same argument over who paid last, who is winning, and who can afford the next sacrifice.
  Source: src-006
  Evidence: repeated collection of the shared rebellion tax structures the same contribution dispute each cycle.
  Confidence: medium

### 16. Table conflict can become personal

- Claim: The system asks groups to distinguish strategic attack, deal enforcement, friendship exploitation, punishment, and spite; not every group can preserve those boundaries.
  Source: src-001, src-006
  Evidence: common ruin is both a strategic lever and a vehicle for denying rivals victory.
  Confidence: medium

### 17. Emotional stubbornness can become bargaining power

- Claim: The player most willing to end the session may control negotiations regardless of strategic merit.
  Source: src-001
  Evidence: the credible ability to throw the game gives uncompromising refusal disproportionate force.
  Confidence: medium

### 18. Optimal play can contradict the fiction

- Claim: Personal scoring may recommend limiting or obstructing the shared mission even when the fiction demands wholehearted commitment to it.
  Source: src-008
  Evidence: indexed *Churchill* discussion asks whether the best move may be not to win the war.
  Confidence: medium

### 19. Hidden objectives can become predictable

- Claim: Memorizing a small objective set can turn suspicion and diplomacy into rote inference.
  Source: src-009
  Evidence: the *Nemesis* review argues that predictable objectives destroy much of the tension and dynamic diplomacy.
  Confidence: high

### 20. Hidden objectives can be unequally hostile

- Claim: One player may draw a goal compatible with normal cooperation while another must pursue severe sabotage, making the draw too influential.
  Source: src-009
  Evidence: adjacent objective examples range from survival-compatible aims to destructive requirements.
  Confidence: medium

### 21. Player elimination is especially painful

- Claim: An eliminated player loses access to both halves of the experience—shared survival and personal competition—while the remaining hybrid game may continue for a long time.
  Source: src-009
  Evidence: *Nemesis* supplies adjacent evidence for lethal semi-cooperative play with continued objectives among survivors.
  Confidence: medium

### 22. The mechanic is difficult to teach honestly

- Claim: “Semi-cooperative” creates incompatible expectations among cooperative, competitive, social, and puzzle-oriented players unless the teacher states that group success is only a prerequisite for individual victory.
  Source: src-002, src-003, src-004
  Evidence: the same *Legendary*-style structure is interpreted by different communities as either cooperative success or a competitive ranking game.
  Confidence: medium

### 23. It requires a narrow player culture

- Claim: The ideal group must accept aggressive negotiation, provisional promises, credible defection, competitive sacrifice, collective defeat, and attacks that remain impersonal.
  Source: src-001, src-006, src-009
  Evidence: the sources jointly document game-throwing, shared-track bargaining, and hidden-objective suspicion.
  Confidence: medium

### 24. Players may house-rule the mechanic out of existence

- Claim: Repeatedly ignoring individual scoring indicates that the common challenge works for the group while the sole-winner layer does not.
  Source: src-003, src-004
  Evidence: *Legendary* is commonly framed and played as a cooperative experience despite formal personal victory points.
  Confidence: medium
